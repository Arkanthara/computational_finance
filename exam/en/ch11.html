Bonds
(Fixed Income)

From a friend's IOU to global financial markets: everything you need to know about debt instruments and their computational valuation.
🎪 Fun context: Imagine your friend Élon asks you for CHF 100, promising to pay you back CHF 5 every 6 months for 2 years, then return your CHF 100. You pull out a contract, write everything down, sign it. Congratulations, you just bought a bond! Now imagine you resell this "IOU" to a third party — the price they agree to pay depends on market rates. If rates have risen since then, they will pay less than CHF 100. This is exactly the essence of the bond market: a fixed contract whose price fluctuates according to the interest rate environment.

§01

Executive Summary — 30 Seconds

⚡ The Essentials
A bond is a debt instrument: the issuer borrows money and contractually commits to paying coupons periodically and repaying the principal at maturity. The cash flow structure is fixed; only the price varies according to market rates. The price is the present value of all future cash flows, and there is an inverse and convex relationship between price and yield. To compare bonds, we use the zero-coupon rate extracted via the Bootstrap algorithm.
§02

Model Oral Exam Answer

A bond is a debt financing vehicle. The issuer, typically a corporation or a state, issues a bond to raise funds; in exchange, it commits to paying periodic coupons and repaying the principal at maturity. The contract is fixed from issuance — only the market price of the bond will evolve.

To value a bond, we compute the present value of all future cash flows, discounted at the required rate of return $y$ — the yield. We obtain the formula $B(t) = \sum_i c_i e^{-y(t_i-t)} + P e^{-y(T-t)}$. If the yield required by the market rises, the bond price falls — this is the inverse relationship. This relationship is also convex: the sensitivity of the price to the yield depends on the level of the latter.

To compare bonds with different structures, we need a common basis: zero-coupon rates. These rates, applicable to bonds with no intermediate coupons, are extracted from market prices via the Bootstrap algorithm: we start with short maturities — where the calculation is direct — then progressively move up using already calculated rates to extract the next ones.

In the market, bonds are distinguished between sovereigns (Govies) and corporates. The yield difference between the two is called the credit spread: it reflects the issuer's default risk, assessed by rating agencies (S&P, Moody's). Finally, bonds expose the investor to several risks: interest rate risk, credit risk, inflation risk, liquidity risk, and reinvestment risk.

§03

Structure of a Bond

Definition and anatomy

A bond is a bilateral debt contract between an issuer (borrower) and a holder (lender/investor). The contract precisely specifies:

Principal

Face Value / Par Value $P$

The nominal amount repaid at maturity. Typically CHF/USD 100. Serves as the base for coupon calculation.

Compensation

Coupon $c_i$

Fixed periodic payment made to the holder. Expressed as a % of the nominal. Frequency: annual, semi-annual, quarterly.

Duration

Maturity $T$

The date on which the issuer repays the principal. Can range from a few months to 30+ years.

Price

Bond Price $B(t)$

The only degree of freedom. Evolves with market conditions, unlike the cash flows which are fixed.

💡 Key Intuition
The cash flow structure is fixed from issuance. What changes is the market price $B(t)$. This is the only degree of freedom: the market adjusts the price until the implicit yield reflects the perceived risk of the issuer.

§04

Bond Valuation

Principle: Present Value of Cash Flows

The value of a bond at time $t$ is the sum of the discounted values of all its future cash flows, discounted at the required rate of return $y$ (the required yield).

Bond pricing formula
$$B(t) = \underbrace{\sum_{i=1}^{n} c_i \cdot e^{-y(t_i - t)}}_{\text{Present value of coupons}} + \underbrace{P \cdot e^{-y(T-t)}}_{\text{Present value of principal}}$$
$B(t)$

Bond price at time $t$

$c_i$

Coupon paid at $t_i$

$y$

Required yield (annualized, continuous)

$P$

Principal (face value), typically 100

$T$

Maturity date

$e^{-y \Delta t}$

Continuous discount factor

Complete numerical example

Bond: 4 coupons of CHF 5 (semi-annual), principal CHF 100, required yield $y = 5\%$.

$c_1 = 5$$t_1 = 0.5$$5 \cdot e^{-0.05 \times 0.5}$CHF 4.88
$c_2 = 5$$t_2 = 1.0$$5 \cdot e^{-0.05 \times 1.0}$CHF 4.76
$c_3 = 5$$t_3 = 1.5$$5 \cdot e^{-0.05 \times 1.5}$CHF 4.64
$c_4 = 5$$t_4 = 2.0$$5 \cdot e^{-0.05 \times 2.0}$CHF 4.52

Since $B = 109.28 > 100$, this bond trades at a premium — the implicit coupon rate (5%/100 = 5%) is higher than the required yield of 5%... which exactly matches the coupon here. The premium is explained by the continuous discounting convention vs. the semi-annual coupon frequency.

⚠️ Continuous vs. discrete compounding
This course uses continuous-time discounting $e^{-y \Delta t}$, unlike the convention $(1+y)^{-n}$ sometimes used elsewhere. The formulas differ but the economic logic is identical.
§05

Iterative Procedure — Trial & Error

The inverse problem: finding y from B

The pricing formula gives $B$ as a function of $y$. In practice, we observe the market price $B$ and seek the implicit yield $y$ — this is the inverse problem. Since $B(y)$ is a non-linear function, there is no analytical solution: we use a numerical iterative procedure (bisection or dichotomy method).

Bisection algorithm

💻 Python Implementation (course)
The course implements this algorithm via a trial_and_error(B) function that : (1) starts with a high $y$, (2) divides $y$ by 2 if the residual is positive (calculated price too low), (3) averages $y$ with the previous iteration if the residual is negative. Convergence guaranteed for a tolerance $|residual| < 10^{-3}$. Verification on the example: for $B = 109.28$, the algorithm converges to $y = 5.00\%$.
§06

Price–Yield Relationship and Convexity

Inverse and convex relationship

The relationship between a bond's price and its yield is inverse (when one rises, the other falls) and exhibits convexity (curvature). This convexity is a fundamental property of bonds.

Practical implications of convexity

Rising rates

⬆️ y → ⬇️ B

A rise in interest rates causes a price decline: capital loss for the investor. Offset if the bond is held to maturity, or by new higher coupons.

Falling rates

⬇️ y → ⬆️ B

A fall in interest rates causes a price increase: capital gain. "Long duration" strategy in anticipation of rate cuts.

Convexity

Favorable asymmetry

Thanks to convexity, the gain from a rate decrease is greater than the loss from an equivalent rate increase. An advantageous property for the investor.

Duration

Sensitivity (next lecture)

Duration measures the sensitivity of price to yield. Long-maturity bonds have higher duration → more sensitive to rate changes.

§07

Zero-Coupon Rates & Bootstrap Algorithm

Why zero-coupon rates?

The yield of a bond with coupons depends on its payment structure. To compare fairly different bonds, we use the zero-coupon rate $r_T$: it is the discount rate applicable to a single payment at date $T$, with no intermediate coupons.

🎯 Definition
A zero-coupon bond pays no coupons; it is simply bought at a discount and repaid at par at maturity. Its price is : $B_{ZC}(t) = P \cdot e^{-r_T (T-t)}$. Zero-coupon rates form the zero-coupon yield curve — the basis of all fixed-income pricing.

Bootstrap Algorithm — Principle

In practice, not all maturities have quoted zero-coupon bonds. We extract zero-coupon rates from the prices of quoted coupon-bearing bonds, proceeding iteratively by increasing maturity.

Resulting zero-coupon yield curve

📐 Shape of the curve
In theory, rates rise with maturity (investors demand a premium for longer durations). In practice, the curve can be inverted (short term > long term, often before a recession), flat, or humped (bump in the middle). The curve must be recalculated regularly as market prices evolve.

Bond Types and Credit Spread

Government vs. Corporate

The main distinction is between sovereign bonds (Govies / Treasuries), issued by states, and corporate bonds. The yield difference between the two for the same maturity is the credit spread.

§10

Role of Bonds in a Portfolio

Diversification

📉 Negative correlation

Bonds have historically a negative or low correlation with equities. When equity markets fall (risk aversion), investors flee to sovereign bonds (flight to quality). Notable exception: 2022, when the sharp rate hike caused both equities and bonds to fall.

Income

💰 Regular cash flows

Coupons provide a predictable and regular income. Attractive for investors with defined cash flow needs (pension funds, insurers, retirees).

Preservation

🛡️ Capital protection

High-quality sovereign bonds (AAA) preserve capital. Principal repayment at maturity is practically guaranteed — fundamental for liability-driven investments.

Allocation

⚖️ Life-cycle investing

The classic rule: allocate (100 − age)% in equities and the rest in bonds. Young investors tolerate more risk; as retirement approaches, the portfolio shifts toward more bonds to reduce volatility.

⚠️ 2022 Exception — Positive equity/bond correlation
In 2022, the Fed and ECB raised rates at a historic pace to fight inflation. Result: bond prices fell simultaneously with equity markets. Bond/equity diversification temporarily failed, reminding us that the correlation regime can change in inflationary stress periods.

§13

Historical Perspective

12th–17th centuries
Medieval origins
Italian republics (Venice, Genoa) issue the first prestiti — government loans with coupons. The Bank of Amsterdam (1609) and the Bank of England (1694) modernize public financing through bonds.
19th century
Modern bond markets
Financing of the Napoleonic wars and the industrial revolution via sovereign and corporate bonds. The Rothschilds dominate the international government loan market.
1973–1980
Oil shock & rate revolution
Galloping inflation pushes bond yields to historic highs. Interest rate risk management becomes critical. Birth of active bond management.
1997
Publication of "Options, Futures & Other Derivatives" — Hull
Formalization of bond pricing and the yield curve. The Bootstrap algorithm enters mainstream academic and practical use.
2008–2009
Global financial crisis
Credit spreads explode. Lehman Brothers' bankruptcy demonstrates the reality of credit risk. Central bank QE (Quantitative Easing) drives sovereign yields toward zero.
2022
Great rate rotation
The Fed raises rates from 0% to 5.5% in 18 months. Worst bond performance in 40 years. Simultaneous equity/bond collapse — questioning the traditional negative correlation.
§14

Possible Exam Questions

● Easy
What is a bond and what are its fundamental components?▾

A bond is a debt instrument: the issuer borrows money and contractually commits to paying coupons periodically and repaying the principal at maturity. Key components: (1) coupon $c_i$ — reward for lending, (2) principal $P$ — amount repaid at maturity $T$, (3) price $B(t)$ — only degree of freedom, (4) yield $y$ — implicit rate of return. The cash flow structure is fixed contractually.

What is the credit spread and how is it interpreted?▾

The credit spread is the yield difference between a corporate bond and a sovereign bond of the same maturity: $\text{CS} = y_{\text{corp}} - y_{\text{sovereign}}$. It represents the risk premium demanded by investors to compensate for the issuer's default risk. The lower the rating (BB, B), the higher the spread. Spread variations reflect changes in the perception of credit risk.

What is the relationship between a bond's price and its yield?▾

The relationship is inverse and convex. Inverse: if the yield rises, the price falls, and vice versa (intuition: future cash flows are discounted at a higher rate, so they are worth less today). Convex: the curve has positive curvature — price sensitivity is not constant. Duration measures the linear sensitivity (tangent), convexity measures the curvature (second-order term).

●● Intermediate
Explain the Bootstrap algorithm step by step with a numerical example.▾

Objective: extract zero-coupon rates $r_T$ from market prices of coupon-bearing bonds.

Step 1 (short maturities, ZC available): If a 6-month ZC is worth 94.9 for a 100 nominal, its rate is $r_{0.5} = 2\ln(1 + 5.1/94.9 \times 2/2) = 10.469\%$.

Step 2 (1.5-year maturity with coupon): Cash flows are 4 at 0.5 year, 4 at 1 year, 104 at 1.5 years. Price = 96. We already know $r_{0.5}$ and $r_{1.0}$. We write: $4 e^{-r_{0.5} \times 0.5} + 4 e^{-r_{1.0} \times 1} + 104 e^{-r_{1.5} \times 1.5} = 96$ and solve for $r_{1.5}$. We obtain $r_{1.5} = 10.681\%$.

Key: we "bootstrap" from short maturities to long ones, using already calculated rates.

A 10-year bond vs. a 2-year bond: which is riskier if rates rise? Why?▾

The 10-year bond is much riskier. The reason is duration (seen in the next lecture): a long-maturity bond has more distant future cash flows that are heavily impacted by discounting. Direct intuition from the pricing formula: the terms $e^{-y \times T}$ with large $T$ are very sensitive to $y$. A 1% yield rise causes the price of a 10-year bond to drop by roughly ~8-9% (duration ≈ 8-9 years) versus ~1.9% for a 2-year bond (duration ≈ 1.9 years).

Why are zero-coupon rates used as a comparison standard?▾

The yields of coupon-bearing bonds depend on their payment structure: a bond with many early coupons has a shorter effective duration, which modifies its yield even for equivalent risk. The zero-coupon rate $r_T$ is pure: it represents the discount rate for a single payment at date $T$, with no ambiguity about intermediate cash flows. It is the universal building block for comparing instruments with different structures and for constructing coherent yield curves.

●●● Difficult
Why is convexity an advantageous property for the investor and how do we profit from it?▾

Convexity implies that gains from a rate decrease are larger than losses from an equivalent rate increase. Mathematically, this is because $B(y)$ is a convex function: the tangent (duration) always underestimates the real price.

To profit from it: "barbell" strategies (very short + very long bonds) have more convexity than a "bullet" (intermediate-maturity bonds) at equal duration. In periods of high rate volatility, convexity has value — we can quantify its value and integrate it into bond option pricing. Traders "long convexity" benefit from large rate fluctuations regardless of direction.

In 2022, equity/bond correlations became positive. What are the implications for portfolio construction?▾

Positive equity/bond correlation breaks the fundamental assumption of the 60/40 portfolio (60% equities, 40% bonds). It occurs when the common driver of decline is inflation: rates rise to fight it, which causes bonds to fall (yield rise) and equities to fall (future dividends discounted at higher rates).<

Implications: (1) bond/equity diversification no longer works as a hedge; (2) investors must seek decorrelated alternatives: commodities, gold, infrastructure, TIPS; (3) bond management must explicitly integrate inflation risk into allocation models; (4) efficient frontier optimization is significantly modified by this regime change.

§15

Classic Exam Pitfalls

⚡
Confusing yield and coupon rate: The coupon rate is set at issuance and never changes. The yield is the implicit rate of return of the current market price — it fluctuates constantly. A "5% coupon" bond can have a yield of 8% if its price has dropped.
⚡
Forgetting convexity: The price-yield relationship is NOT linear. The linear approximation (duration alone) systematically underestimates the real price — the approximation error is the convexity term. Citing only duration without mentioning convexity is incomplete.
⚡
Confusing "at a premium" and "overvalued": A bond trading above par (at a premium, B > 100) is not overvalued — it simply means its coupon rate is higher than the market yield. This is a perfectly normal situation.
⚡
Thinking Bootstrap only applies to ZC: The Bootstrap algorithm applies precisely when bonds have coupons. For pure ZC bonds, the calculation is direct. The added value of Bootstrap is to process COUPON-BEARING bonds to extract ZC rates.
⚡
Neglecting the order of Bootstrap application: One must imperatively proceed by increasing maturity. We cannot calculate $r_{1.5}$ without knowing $r_{0.5}$ and $r_{1.0}$ — this is the recursive nature of the algorithm.
⚡
Confusing interest rate risk and credit risk: These are two distinct risks. A rise in sovereign rates impacts all bonds (including sovereigns). Credit risk is specific to the issuer. Both can occur simultaneously and amplify each other in crises.
⚡
Assuming the yield curve is always upward sloping: In practice, the curve can be inverted (2007, 2022), flat, or humped. An inverted curve is often interpreted as a signal of an impending recession.

Final Revision Sheet

🔑 Essential Definitions

  • Bond — Debt contract: fixed coupons + principal at maturity
  • Coupon $c_i$ — Fixed periodic payment set at issuance
  • Yield $y$ — Implicit rate of return of the market price
  • Zero-coupon rate $r_T$ — Rate for a single payment at $T$
  • Credit spread CS — $y_{\text{corp}} - y_{\text{sovereign}}$
  • At premium/par/discount — $B > = <$ 100

📐 Key Formulas

  • Price — $B(t) = \sum c_i e^{-y(t_i-t)} + Pe^{-y(T-t)}$
  • ZC price — $B_{ZC} = Pe^{-r_T(T-t)}$
  • ZC rate — $r_T = -\frac{1}{T}\ln(B_{ZC}/P)$
  • Bootstrap — Solve for $r_T$ after subtracting known CF
  • Yield decomposition — $y = y_{sov} + CS$

🔄 Bootstrap Algorithm

  • 1. Sort bonds by increasing maturity
  • 2. If ZC: calculate $r_T$ directly
  • 3. If coupon: use known $r$ for intermediate CF
  • 4. Isolate $e^{-r_T T}$ and deduce $r_T$
  • 5. Repeat for next maturity
  • ⚠️ Increasing order mandatory (recursive)

⚖️ Key Relationships

  • ⬆️ Yield ⇒ ⬇️ Price (inverse)
  • Convexity = curvature of B(y), favorable to investor
  • Long duration = high sensitivity to rates
  • Low rating = high credit spread = high yield
  • ZC bond = no reinvestment risk

⚠️ 5 Risks to Remember

  • Rate — Rate rise → price fall
  • Credit — Issuer default → capital loss
  • Inflation — Purchasing power erosion
  • Liquidity — Narrow secondary market
  • Reinvestment — Coupons reinvested at low rates

📊 Role in the portfolio

  • Diversification — Negative correlation with equities (usually)
  • Income — Regular and predictable cash flows
  • Preservation — Capital repaid at maturity
  • Life-cycle — Bond proportion rises with age
  • ⚠️ 2022 — Positive correlation notable exception
🎯 Central message to remember for the oral exam
A bond is a fixed cash flow contract whose valuation relies on discounting. Price and yield maintain an inverse and convex relationship. To compare heterogeneous bonds, we use zero-coupon rates extracted via the Bootstrap algorithm. Yield decomposes into sovereign rate + credit spread. Bonds play an essential role in diversification and income generation in a portfolio, provided the five associated risks are actively managed.
