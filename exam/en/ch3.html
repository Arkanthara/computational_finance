The Story of the Blind Trader

📖 New York, May 2010 — 2:42 PM

Thomas is an algorithmic trader at a Manhattan hedge fund. For 8 years, his quantitative model has performed regularly. He calibrated his algorithm on a simple assumption: prices follow a Gaussian random walk. Volatility is constant, distribution tails are negligible.

This Monday afternoon, his screen displays something impossible. The Dow Jones just lost 998 points in 5 minutes. Not an hour. Five minutes. A 9.2% instantaneous drop. His algorithm, which had never modeled such an event — because the Gaussian told him it was statistically impossible — keeps buying. It accumulates losing positions at a terrifying speed.

In 20 minutes, the markets recover. But Thomas has lost millions. Not because he was stupid. Because he ignored three fundamental things: markets have fat tails, volatility clusters, and endogenous events that no Gaussian model can capture.

This chapter gives you the tools to not be Thomas.

Context

This chapter addresses financial markets from the empirical and behavioral angle — two dimensions often neglected by purely theoretical models. The central issue: understanding to what extent real markets deviate from an ideal random walk, and what regular structures (stylised facts) can be extracted from them.

Chapter Overview

Executive Summary (30 seconds): This chapter examines the Efficient Market Hypothesis (Efficient Market Hypothesis), the structure of trading venues, asset classes and instruments, and stylised facts — that is, robust empirical statistical properties that distinguish a real financial series from a simple random walk. The main conclusion: markets are not Gaussian random walks.

01

Efficient Market Hypothesis (EMH)

Definition & Intuition

The Efficient Market Hypothesis (Efficient Market Hypothesis, EMH) was proposed by Eugene Fama in 1960 (Nobel Prize 2013). It rests on a fundamentally simple idea: if agents are rational and if information circulates freely, then the price of an asset always reflects all available information.

Central Implication
It is impossible to beat the market systematically. Every asset is always at its fair value. Impossible to buy undervalued or sell overvalued.

The 3 Forms of EMH

Pyramid of the 3 Forms of EMH
FormInformation IncorporatedConsequenceValidated?
WeakPast prices onlyTechnical analysis useless · Price = random walkContested
Semi-strongAll public info (announcements, reports…)Fundamental analysis uselessVery contested
StrongPublic + private info (insider trading)Even insiders cannot beat the marketRefuted

Critiques of EMH

The theory is widely criticized, even in its weak form. Several empirical phenomena challenge it:

Behavior

Herding

Agents imitate the crowd rather than acting on their own information. Creates irrational trends.

Anomaly

Speculative Bubbles

Bitcoin 2013, dot-com 2000: prices durably deviate from fundamental value.

Technology

Algorithmic Trading

Algos create feedback loops that amplify movements — Flash Crash of May 6, 2010.

Managers

Soros & Buffett

Beating the market for 50 years is not just luck. The probability of that is astronomically low.

Case Study: Flash Crash (May 6, 2010)

Flash Crash · DOW Jones · May 6, 2010 · Shock Simulation
Nobel Paradox 2013
Eugene Fama (EMH) and Robert Shiller (irrational markets) shared the same Nobel Prize. Economics is the only discipline where two diametrically opposed theories can be rewarded simultaneously. This illustrates the lack of consensus on efficiency.

Model oral answer: "The EMH, proposed by Fama in 1960, states that prices reflect at every instant all available information. It exists in three forms: weak — only past prices are incorporated —, semi-strong — all public information is incorporated immediately —, and strong — even private information. The theoretical consequence is the impossibility of beating the market systematically.

However, this hypothesis is strongly criticized. Phenomena such as the Flash Crash of 2010 — where the Dow Jones fell 9.2% in five minutes before recovering — or speculative bubbles on Bitcoin illustrate that markets can adopt deeply irrational behaviors. Herd behavior, algorithmic trading, and fat tails of return distributions fundamentally challenge the Gaussian paradigm on which EMH rests."

Stylised Facts

Stylised Facts designate a set of robust and universal empirical statistical properties of financial series. They are observed in virtually all markets, across all time scales. These are the fundamental differences between real prices and a simple random walk.

Central Academic Reference
R. Cont, "Empirical properties of asset returns: stylized facts and statistical issues", Quantitative Finance, 2001.

① Volatility Clustering (Heteroskedasticity)

Intuition: Large price variations are followed by large variations, and small variations by small variations. Volatility is not constant over time — it clusters.

Simulation: Volatility Clustering vs Gaussian Random Walk
GARCH(1,1) Model — capturing clustering
$$\sigma_t^2 = \omega + \alpha \epsilon_{t-1}^2 + \beta \sigma_{t-1}^2$$

Where $\epsilon_{t-1}^2$ is the past shock, $\sigma_{t-1}^2$ the past variance. The term $\beta$ captures the persistence of volatility.

② Fat Tails — Leptokurtosis

Intuition: "Extreme" events (brutal drops, sudden rises) are much more frequent than predicted by a Gaussian distribution. The normal model dramatically underestimates the probability of crashes.

Fat Tails vs Gaussian Distribution
Critical Implication — Underestimated Value at Risk
If we model returns with a Gaussian, we dramatically underestimate the probability of a -10% or greater crash. Thomas's model (the story at the beginning) made exactly this error. Lévy or Student-t distributions are more appropriate for capturing fat tails.

③ Negative Auto-correlation in the Short Term

At high frequency (tick data), we observe a negative short-term auto-correlation in price changes. Intuition: if a price jumps up (buyer shock), there is a tendency for a slight correction in the following ticks. This phenomenon is linked to the bid-ask bounce — transactions alternate between ask and bid.

Price change auto-correlation — high frequency

④ Scaling Laws

Markets exhibit similarities at different time scales. The expected absolute return follows a power law as a function of the time interval $\Delta t$:

Empirical scaling law
$$\mathbb{E}\left[|r|\right] \sim \Delta t^{\alpha}$$

$r$ : return over interval $\Delta t$ · $\alpha$ : scaling exponent (≈ 0.5 for Brownian motion, but different in practice) · This relationship is linear on a log-log plot.

For standard Brownian motion, $\alpha = 0.5$ (since volatility scales as $\sqrt{\Delta t}$). Deviations from this behavior reveal long memory or non-linear effects in real markets.

⑤ Other Stylised Facts

Stylised FactDescriptionModeling implication
Volatility Clustering Large/small variations cluster in time · Heteroskedasticity → Use GARCH, EGARCH
Fat Tails Thick tails · Excess kurtosis · Leptokurtosis → Lévy, Student-t distributions
Negative autocorrelation Negative correlation at short term (high frequency) → Microstructure, bid-ask bounce
Scaling Laws $\mathbb{E}[|r|] \sim \Delta t^\alpha$ → Multifractal models
Asymmetry (equity) Drops are stronger than rises (leverage effect) → Volatility negatively correlated with returns
Spread/volume correlation The tighter the spread, the higher the volume → Microstructure models
Volume/volatility correlation Strong positive correlation between volume and volatility → Liquidity models
Central message to remember for the oral exam
The set of these stylised facts converges toward one conclusion: financial markets are not Gaussian random walks. They have memory, fat-tailed distributions, time-varying volatility, and recurrent structures. Any model that ignores these properties (like Black-Scholes with constant σ) will be structurally biased.

Exam Questions

Easy
What is the difference between a Market Maker and a Broker?▾
A Market Maker (e.g., Oanda) proposes bid/ask prices, holds inventory and therefore takes market risk — it is remunerated via the spread. A Broker (e.g., Dukascopy) takes no risk, holds no inventory, and transmits orders while earning a commission. The key distinction: inventory = risk = market maker.
List the 3 forms of EMH and their meaning.▾
Weak: past prices are incorporated → technical analysis is useless. Semi-strong: all public information is incorporated immediately → fundamental analysis is useless. Strong: even private information (insider) is incorporated → no one can beat the market systematically.
What is a Futures and how does it differ from a Forward?▾
A Forward is a bilateral OTC contract, non-standardized, between two parties, to buy/sell an asset at a fixed price and date. A Futures is the standardized version of a forward, traded on an exchange with a clearing process. The Futures reduces counterparty risk thanks to the clearing house that substitutes itself for both parties.
Intermediate
What is volatility clustering? Which model captures it and how?▾
Volatility clustering is the phenomenon by which large price variations concentrate in time (followed by large variations), and likewise for small variations. It is a property of conditional heteroskedasticity — variance is not constant. The GARCH(1,1) model captures it: $\sigma_t^2 = \omega + \alpha\epsilon_{t-1}^2 + \beta\sigma_{t-1}^2$. The $\beta$ term (persistence) close to 1 indicates long memory of volatility. This is the "H" in GARCH: Heteroskedastic.
Why do FX markets have a liquidity peak in the early afternoon (European time)?▾
Because it is the London-New York overlap window (approximately 1:00 PM–5:00 PM GMT). During this period, the two most important financial centers in the world operate simultaneously. The superposition of order books from both venues maximizes liquidity, reduces spreads, and increases volume. This phenomenon is well documented in S. Masry (2011) and represents the daily peak of global FX activity.
What is the difference between an endogenous and an exogenous event? Give an example of each.▾
An endogenous event arises from the internal dynamics of the market (e.g., Flash Crash of May 6, 2010 — stop-loss cascade triggered by algorithms, with no external news). An exogenous event is information or a shock coming from outside (e.g., SNB EUR/CHF floor imposed in September 2011 — surprise monetary policy decision). The distinction is important for modeling: endogenous events are partially capturable by microstructure models, exogenous ones require jump models.
Difficult
How do fat tails challenge Black-Scholes, and what are the alternatives?▾
Black-Scholes rests on the assumption that log-returns follow a normal (Gaussian) distribution. However, fat tails show that the empirical kurtosis of returns is greater than 3 (leptokurtic). Consequences: (1) the risk of extreme drops is underestimated — leading to a dangerously low Value-at-Risk; (2) implied volatility varies with the strike (volatility smile), which BS cannot capture with a constant σ.

Alternatives: fat-tailed distributions such as the Lévy-stable distribution (Mandelbrot, 1963), the Student-t distribution, jump models (Merton 1976, Kou 2002), or stochastic volatility models (Heston). These approaches capture excess kurtosis and the volatility smile.
Why was the 2013 Nobel Prize shared between Fama and Shiller when their theories are opposed? What does this say about EMH?▾
Fama (EMH) defends the idea that prices incorporate all available information and that markets cannot be predicted. Shiller, on the contrary, demonstrates that asset prices are excessively volatile relative to fundamentals (discounted dividends), implying persistent irrationality. The Nobel Committee rewarded both because they both contributed to understanding price formation: Fama in the short term (hard to predict), Shiller in the long term (partial predictability via P/E ratios). This illustrates that the efficiency question is fundamentally unresolved, and probably poorly posed as binary: markets can be partially efficient, at different frequencies and for different asset classes.

Classic Pitfalls

⚠️
Confusing Forward and Futures: A Forward is bilateral and OTC (non-standardized); a Futures is standardized and traded on an exchange with clearing. Never say "a futures is just a forward traded on an exchange" without mentioning standardization and the clearing process.
⚠️
Believing that weak EMH = pure random markets: The weak form says past prices contain no exploitable information, but it does not exclude fat tails, volatility clustering, or stylised facts. A market can be weakly efficient AND have fat tails.
⚠️
Underestimating Fat Tails: A -5% drop in one day is predicted as "impossible" by the Gaussian (50 standard deviations), but happens several times per decade. Using a Gaussian for extreme risk is a fundamental modeling error.
⚠️
Confusing seasonality and trend: Seasonality is a recurrent and predictable variation (intraday, weekly, monthly). Trend is a directional long-term evolution. A model must treat them separately.
⚠️
Reversing endogenous/exogenous: Endogenous = internal origin (liquidity, algos). Exogenous = external origin (news, central banks). Do not confuse them, especially for the Flash Crash (endogenous!) and the SNB floor (exogenous!).
⚠️
Forgetting roll-over on Futures: In practice, commodity futures are sold/bought before maturity and replaced by the next contract. We don't want to take delivery of a ton of wheat. This mechanism creates the roll yield which can be positive (backwardation) or negative (contango).
