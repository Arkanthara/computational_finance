Data, Moving Average
& Intrinsic Time

From noisy tick data to the scaling laws of high-frequency markets: signal filtering, adaptive time, and a new way to read price dynamics.

00

Executive Summary

30-second brief

Real financial data are irregular, noisy, and non-continuous — the opposite of what we naively model. To extract the trend, we use moving averages (SMA, WMA, EMA), which smooth the signal at the cost of an inevitable time lag. The EMA is the most efficient in production (O(1) update). But physical time itself is arbitrary: a radical alternative, intrinsic time, measures time by price directional changes (directional changes), capturing the true dynamics regardless of the clock. Scaling laws (scaling laws) bridge the two paradigms.

Core Problem

Imperfect Data

Non-equidistant, noisy, with bid/ask spread. Discretization and interpolation are the two standard responses, each imperfect.

Smoothing

Moving Averages

SMA: simple, heavy memory. WMA: linear weights. EMA: exponential weights, O(1), parameter α controls reactivity ↔ smoothing.

Alternative

Intrinsic Time

Time advances only on market events (DC events). Captures volatility and liquidity without depending on physical time.

Empirical Validation

Scaling Laws

Power-law relations between threshold δ and market properties. Robust across 13+ FX currency pairs.

01

Model Oral Answer

This chapter deals with the reality of financial data and the tools to analyze them. In computational finance, we tend to idealize data — we imagine it smooth, equidistant, continuous. Reality is very different: market data are irregular tick data, with a bid/ask spread, and activity that varies considerably depending on the hour or day.

To smooth this noise, we have three major families of moving averages. The Simple Moving Average (SMA) computes a uniform average over a sliding window of $n$ observations — simple, but it gives the same weight to an observation one month old and yesterday's. The Weighted Moving Average (WMA) corrects this by assigning increasing linear weights. But the most used in practice is the Exponential Moving Average (EMA), which can be updated in O(1) via the recurrence $\text{EMA}(t) = \alpha X_t + (1-\alpha)\,\text{EMA}(t-1)$. The parameter $\alpha$ controls the trade-off between reactivity and smoothing. A large $\alpha$ = reactive but noisy. A small $\alpha$ = smooth but with a strong time lag.

All these approaches share a fundamental flaw: they remain attached to physical time, which is an arbitrary measure for markets. Markets do not "live" at the same speed at night and during trading sessions. This is where the concept of intrinsic time comes in. The idea is simple and elegant: we only advance time when the market does something significant — namely, a price directional change of more than $\delta$. These events are called directional changes. Between two DCs, the price continues in the same direction — this is the overshoot. The sum DC + overshoot forms the total move.

This framework is not purely theoretical. We can measure remarkably robust scaling laws that relate the threshold $\delta$ to observable quantities such as the average number of ticks, the duration of DCs, or the size of overshoots. These laws are verified on 13 FX currency pairs with remarkably stable exponents. They allow us to calibrate models and interpret market microstructure.

02

Real Data in Finance

What we imagine vs. what exists

Naive intuition represents price as a smooth, continuous curve sampled at regular intervals. Market reality is radically different: data arrive as ticks at irregular intervals, each representing a transaction or a quote. We simultaneously observe a bid (price offered to buy), an ask (price offered to sell), and a mid (the midpoint between the two).

Fig. 1 — The naive hypothesis vs. the reality of tick data in market finance.

How to handle them?

Faced with these imperfect data, two approaches exist:

Discretization (dominant approach — 99.9% of cases)

We fix a temporal grid (e.g., 1 min, 1 hour) and keep only the instantaneous price at these moments. Advantage: simple, standard, compatible with all classical models. Disadvantage: information between ticks is lost, intra-period trends go unnoticed.

Interpolation (rare usage)

We use past observations to infer values between ticks. Advantage: more information is used. Disadvantage: strong assumptions, and physical time remains a rigid and arbitrary measure.

03

Simple Moving Average (SMA)

The simple moving average (Simple Moving Average) is the first response to the noise problem: we replace the instantaneous value with the average of the last $n$ observations.

Definition — SMA over window n
$$\text{SMA}(t) = \frac{X_{t-n+1} + X_{t-n+2} + \cdots + X_t}{n} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1} X_{t-k}$$

Recursive update

The computational trick of the SMA is that it can be updated incrementally by adding the new observation and subtracting the oldest one:

$$\text{SMA}(t+1) = \text{SMA}(t) - \frac{X_{t-n+1}}{n} + \frac{X_{t+1}}{n}$$
Two fundamental problems of SMA

1. Equal weighting of information: an observation $n$ steps old has exactly the same weight ($1/n$) as a recent observation. This is economically questionable — recent information should count more.

2. Inevitable time lag: the larger $n$, the more the average smooths, but the greater the delay relative to the real signal. This trade-off is fundamental and cannot be eliminated.

Fig. 2 — The SMA smooths effectively, but introduces an inherent time lag: the moving average peak appears after the real peak. The larger $n$, the greater the lag.

Memory cost

The SMA requires keeping the last $n$ observations in memory. For a large $n$ on high-frequency data, this can represent a significant cost.

04

Weighted Moving Average (WMA)

To correct the equal weighting of the SMA, we assign increasing weights to recent observations: the most recent observation receives weight $n$, the oldest receives weight $1$.

Definition — WMA
$$\text{WMA}(t) = \frac{1 \cdot X_{t-n+1} + 2 \cdot X_{t-n+2} + \cdots + n \cdot X_t}{1 + 2 + \cdots + n} = \frac{\sum_{k=1}^{n} k \cdot X_{t-n+k}}{\dfrac{n(n+1)}{2}}$$

Recursive update (more complex)

Updating the WMA requires two auxiliary quantities:

$$\text{Total}(t) = \sum_{k=0}^{n-1} X_{t-k}$$ $$\text{Total}(t+1) = \text{Total}(t) - X_{t-n+1} + X_{t+1}$$ $$\text{WMA}(t+1) = \frac{\text{WMA}(t) \cdot \tfrac{n(n+1)}{2} + n \cdot X_{t+1} - \text{Total}(t)}{\tfrac{n(n+1)}{2}}$$
Main drawback

The update is significantly more complex than for the SMA. We must maintain an auxiliary sum Total(t). The algebraic structure is heavier to implement and maintain.

Fig. 3 — Compared weighting schemes: SMA (uniform), WMA (linear), EMA (exponential). In all three cases, the rightmost bar corresponds to the most recent observation $t$.

05

Exponential Moving Average (EMA)

The EMA is the most widely used moving average in practice. Its major advantage: it combines an exponential weighting scheme (closer to economic reality) with an O(1) update — without memory of past observations.

Intuitive derivation

We first define the "infinite" form of the EMA with exponential weights:

Expanded form
$$\text{EMA}(t) = \frac{X_t + (1-\alpha)X_{t-1} + (1-\alpha)^2 X_{t-2} + \cdots}{1 + (1-\alpha) + (1-\alpha)^2 + \cdots}$$

Using the geometric series $\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}(1-\alpha)^i = 1/\alpha$, we can factor:

Fundamental EMA recurrence
$$\boxed{\text{EMA}(t) = \alpha \cdot X_t + (1-\alpha) \cdot \text{EMA}(t-1)}$$
Why it's remarkable

This simple recurrence only needs two values: the current observation $X_t$ and the previous EMA. No need to store the past $n$ observations. It's a first-order infinite impulse response (IIR) filter — elegant and efficient.

Choosing the α parameter

There are two main approaches to calibrate α:

Method 1 — Average age equivalence with SMA(n)

We equalize the weighted average age of an EMA and an SMA of size $n$:

$$\sum_{i=0}^{\infty} i \cdot (1-\alpha)^i \cdot \alpha = \frac{(1-\alpha)}{\alpha} \stackrel{!}{=} \frac{n-1}{2} \quad\Rightarrow\quad \boxed{\alpha = \frac{2}{n+1}}$$

Attention: this equivalence concerns only the average age. SMA(n) ≠ EMA with $\alpha = 2/(n+1)$ in general.

Method 2 — Residual impact after k steps

We choose α such that an observation $X_{t-k}$ has residual impact $\epsilon_k$:

$$(1-\alpha)^k = \epsilon_k \quad\Rightarrow\quad \alpha = 1 - e^{\frac{\ln(\epsilon_k)}{k}}$$

Example: for $k=100$ and $\epsilon_k = 1\%$: $\alpha = 0.045$. For SMA(100): $\alpha = 2/101 \approx 0.020$.

Initial conditions

InitializationFormulaAdvantageDrawback
Cold start$\text{EMA}(0) = 0$ or $\text{EMA}(1) = X_1$SimpleStrong initial instability
Bootstrap SMA$\text{EMA}(n) = \text{SMA}(n)$Reduced warm-up periodRequires $n$ observations

In all cases, one must wait for the EMA to "settle" — typically several multiples of $1/\alpha$ steps before using the value.

06

EMA for Asynchronous Data

The SMA and classic EMA assume regularly spaced observations. In high-frequency finance, ticks arrive irregularly. The standard EMA applied naively would treat a 30-minute gap the same as a 30-second gap. An extension is needed that takes into account the real time between observations.

Zumbach & Müller (2001) construction

We define a temporally adaptive EMA operator via two parameters:

Fundamental parameters
$$\alpha = \frac{t_n - t_{n-1}}{\tau} \qquad \mu = e^{-\alpha} \qquad \nu = \frac{1-\mu}{\alpha}$$
Intuitive interpretation
  • $\tau$ : reference time constant (free parameter)
  • $\alpha$ : fraction of time elapsed since the last observation (normalized by $\tau$)
  • $\mu = e^{-\alpha}$ : forgetting factor — if $\alpha$ is large (long silence), $\mu \to 0$, the EMA "forgets" more
  • $\nu$ : interpolation coefficient between the old and new observation
Asynchronous EMA operator
$$\text{EMA}(\tau; x)(t_n) = \mu \cdot \text{EMA}(\tau; x)(t_{n-1}) + (\nu - \mu) \cdot x_{n-1} + (1-\nu) \cdot x_n$$

Fig. 4 — The asynchronous EMA adapts its "forgetting" to the real time elapsed. A long silence between two ticks (weekend, market closure) gives a large α → μ ≈ 0 → the EMA almost entirely ignores the past.

Derived operators

This framework allows building more sophisticated operators:

Iterated EMA (order-n smoothing)
$$\text{EMA}(\tau, n; x) = \text{EMA}(\tau; \text{EMA}(\tau, n-1; x))$$
SMA from EMAs
$$\text{SMA}(\tau, n) = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n} \text{EMA}\left(\tau'; k\right) \quad\text{with}\quad \tau' = \frac{2\tau}{n+1}$$
07

Limits of Physical Time

Physical time is the implicit reference framework of all classical financial analysis. But is it really the right measure of market time?

Fundamental problems of physical time
  • Arbitrary: when do we start observing? At midnight? At 5 PM? This choice affects statistics.
  • Heterogeneous: market activity varies enormously — zero at night, strong during sessions, almost zero on weekends. One "hour" is worth as much at night as during trading.
  • Missing events: if the sampling interval is larger than the duration of an important event, that event disappears from the data.
  • Asynchronous multi-market data: markets in Tokyo, London, and New York have offset activities — comparing them in physical time is problematic.

What alternatives?

Idea 1

Volume Time

Time advances one unit for every volume $V$ traded. Problem: exact volume is only known a posteriori.

Idea 2

Transaction Count

One unit of time = a certain number of transactions. Same problem: known after the fact.

Idea 3 ✓

Intrinsic Time

Based on price only — always available in real time. Time advances on significant directional changes.

08

Directional Change & Overshoot

Intrinsic time (intrinsic time) is defined by occurrences of a particular type of event: the directional change (DC). The intuition is simple — the market "speaks" when it changes direction significantly.

Price movement decomposition

Key definitions
  • Directional Change (DC): trend reversal exceeding threshold $\delta$ since the last extremum. This is the event that "confirms" a turnaround.
  • Overshoot (OS): continuation of the movement in the new direction after the DC and until the next extremum. This is the "overshoot" once the trend is confirmed.
  • Total Move (TM): $\text{TM} = \text{DC} + \text{OS}$. The total movement between two consecutive extrema.
  • Extremum ($x_\text{ext}$): maximum (or minimum) price reached before a DC is triggered.

Fig. 5 — Architecture of intrinsic time. The total movement (TM) between two extrema decomposes into DC (the portion "confirming" the reversal) and OS (the subsequent overshoot). Intrinsic time (bottom) only advances at each DC event, unlike physical time (regular).

Fundamental properties

PropertyRelationInterpretation
Number of DCs ↑ when δ ↓ The smaller δ, the more directional changes are detected
DC ↔ Volatility Nb DC / physical time ↑ when volatility ↑ Intrinsic time "accelerates" when the market is agitated
OS ↔ Liquidity Long OS → low liquidity A large overshoot suggests a lack of counterparties to stop the trend
TM = DC + OS $\text{TM}^\text{up} + \text{TM}^\text{down} \approx 2\text{OS}$ (empirical) The overshoot is on average comparable to the DC itself
09

DC Detection Algorithm

The algorithm maintains a state (mode = UP or DOWN) and a current extremum (x_ext). At each new price, it checks whether a DC has occurred.

Fig. 6 — Directional Change event detection algorithm. The state (mode) alternates between UP and DOWN at each detected DC. The threshold $\delta$ controls sensitivity: small δ → more DCs (finer intrinsic time).

Algorithm details

A few crucial points to understand for the oral exam:

  • Initialization: arbitrary mode (e.g. "up"), $x_\text{ext}$ initialized to the first price.
  • Symmetry: the algorithm is symmetric — the condition for a DC UP is $(x - x_\text{ext})/x_\text{ext} \geq \delta$ (rise from trough), and for a DC DOWN: $(x_\text{ext} - x)/x_\text{ext} \geq \delta$ (fall from peak).
  • O(1) per tick: the algorithm only looks at a single price at a time and only keeps the state and current extremum — very efficient in high frequency.
  • Invariance of start: the initial "up" or "down" mode only has a transient impact — after a few DCs, the algorithm converges to the same behavior regardless of the starting point.
10

Scaling Laws

Once DCs and OSs are defined, we can study how their statistical properties vary with threshold δ. We discover remarkable empirical relations: robust power laws between δ and various market quantities. These scaling laws form the bridge between intrinsic time and physical time.

Examples of fundamental scaling laws

Law 1 — Average price movement as a function of time
$$\langle \Delta x \rangle \sim \Delta t^{\,\alpha} \quad \text{(generalized diffusion law)}$$
Law 2 — Average DC time as a function of δ
$$\langle \Delta t_\text{dc} \rangle \sim \left(\frac{\delta}{c}\right)^{\!\alpha}$$

Fig. 7 — Tick-count scaling law on a log-log plot. All FX currency pairs align on the same power-law line, indicating universal market behavior. The slope β measures the power-law exponent.

Catalog of empirical scaling laws (Glattfelder et al., 2011)

The reference paper [2] identifies no fewer than 12 distinct scaling laws, all verified on 13 FX currency pairs:

Why it's remarkable

These laws are non-trivial: they apply across orders of magnitude of δ, on different markets (EUR/USD, GBP/JPY, etc.), and their exponents are stable and reproducible. This suggests an underlying fractal structure in FX price dynamics — a universal characteristic of market microstructure.

Possible Exam Questions

Easy level
What is the fundamental difference between SMA and EMA in terms of weighting scheme? ▾

The SMA assigns a uniform weight $1/n$ to each of the last $n$ observations — an observation from yesterday and one from a month ago have exactly the same weight. The EMA assigns exponentially decreasing weights $\alpha(1-\alpha)^k$ — the most recent observation receives weight $\alpha$, the previous one $(1-\alpha)\alpha$, etc. The EMA therefore better reflects the economic intuition that recent information is more relevant. Bonus: the EMA can be updated in O(1) (a single scalar in memory), unlike the SMA which must maintain a window of $n$ observations.

What is a time lag in the context of moving averages? Is it avoidable? ▾

The time lag is the delay between an event in the raw series and its repercussion in the moving average. For example, if the price reaches a peak at $t=100$, the SMA(20) will only reach its peak about 10 steps later. This lag is inevitable and fundamental for any causal moving average (using only the past): the more we smooth, the more we delay. There is an irreducible trade-off between smoothing (noise eliminated) and reactivity (low lag). For the EMA: large $\alpha$ = little lag but little smoothing; small $\alpha$ = strong smoothing but strong lag. We choose according to the application.

Why are real financial data not smooth and equidistant? ▾

Financial data are composed of ticks — each transaction or quote update generates a record. These ticks arrive irregularly because market activity is not constant: intense during sessions (especially at overlap hours between geographic zones), almost zero at night or on weekends. Furthermore, we observe a bid/ask spread: the "buyer" price (bid) and "seller" price (ask) are not identical, so there is not a single price but at least two. The mid series (average of the two) is a construction, not a direct datum. Noise comes from market imperfections (price granularity, spread impact) and microstructure.

Intermediate level
Explain the recurrence $\text{EMA}(t) = \alpha X_t + (1-\alpha)\text{EMA}(t-1)$. How do we derive it? ▾

We start from the definition with exponential weights: $\text{EMA}(t) = \frac{X_t + (1-\alpha)X_{t-1} + (1-\alpha)^2X_{t-2}+\cdots}{1 + (1-\alpha) + (1-\alpha)^2+\cdots}$. The denominator is an infinite geometric series $= 1/\alpha$. We can rewrite the numerator:

$X_t + (1-\alpha)[X_{t-1} + (1-\alpha)X_{t-2}+\cdots] = X_t + (1-\alpha) \cdot \frac{1}{\alpha}\cdot\text{EMA}(t-1)$

Therefore: $\text{EMA}(t) = \frac{X_t + (1-\alpha)/\alpha \cdot \text{EMA}(t-1)}{1/\alpha} = \alpha X_t + (1-\alpha)\text{EMA}(t-1)$.

This simple recurrence says: the current EMA is a linear weighted interpolation between the new observation and the previous EMA. Economically: we "forget" a fraction $\alpha$ of the past and incorporate $\alpha$ of the new information.

Why is physical time "arbitrary" for financial markets? What does intrinsic time propose instead? ▾

Physical time is arbitrary for several reasons: (1) when to start observing? At midnight UTC? At the start of the New York session? This choice affects statistics. (2) Market activity is strongly heterogeneous in time: one hour at night represents almost zero activity, one hour at 2 PM in London can represent thousands of transactions. Treating these two hours as equivalent is a strong assumption. (3) Important events can occur between two sampling instants and be invisible.

Intrinsic time proposes that time only advances on significant market events: Directional Changes (reversals of δ%). The rhythm of intrinsic time is therefore dictated by the market itself — it accelerates when the market is volatile (many DCs), it slows down when the market is calm. Moreover, it uses only price, available in real time.

What does the Overshoot (OS) represent in intrinsic time? What market property does it measure? ▾

The Overshoot is the continuation of the price movement in the direction of a DC after the DC has been confirmed, until the next reversal occurs. Concretely: if the price falls by 2% (DC DOWN confirmed), then continues to drop by 3% before rising — this OS of 3% is the "overshoot". The Total Move (TM) = DC + OS = 2% + 3% = 5%.

The OS measures market liquidity: a long OS means there are few counterparties willing to buy (in the case of a decline) — the market must "search" for buyers lower down. A very liquid market will have short OSs because opposing orders exist at every level. Conversely, the frequency of DCs (or the duration of DCs) measures volatility: the more often the market reverses, the denser the intrinsic time.

Hard level
Why does the formula $\alpha = 2/(n+1)$ not make SMA(n) equal to the EMA with this α? What is equal? ▾

The formula $\alpha = 2/(n+1)$ is derived by equalizing the weighted average age of observations in both cases. For an SMA(n), the average age is $(n-1)/2$. For the EMA, the average age is $\sum_{i=0}^{\infty} i \cdot \alpha(1-\alpha)^i = (1-\alpha)/\alpha$. Solving $(1-\alpha)/\alpha = (n-1)/2$ gives $\alpha = 2/(n+1)$.

But equal average ages are not enough to make the two filters identical: the weight distribution is fundamentally different (uniform vs. exponential). The SMA has a sharp cutoff at $n$ observations (zero weight beyond), the EMA has an infinite exponential decline. Their frequency responses are therefore different. The formula $\alpha = 2/(n+1)$ is a practical calibration convention, not an exact equivalence.

Explain the asynchronous EMA operator: why do we need ν, and what does it represent mathematically? ▾

In the asynchronous case, between $t_{n-1}$ and $t_n$, we do not know how the price evolved. The formula $\text{EMA}(t_n) = \mu \cdot \text{EMA}(t_{n-1}) + (\nu-\mu)x_{n-1} + (1-\nu)x_n$ uses two coefficients:

$\mu = e^{-\alpha}$ is the forgetting factor — it translates the exponential depreciation of the EMA during the absence of data (as if the EMA "decayed" naturally between ticks). For a very long silence, $\mu \to 0$: the EMA loses all memory.

$\nu = (1-\mu)/\alpha$ is the interpolation coefficient. Mathematically, it represents the integrated contribution of $x_{n-1}$ between $t_{n-1}$ and $t_n$, assuming the price remained constant at $x_{n-1}$ during the silence period. When $\alpha \to 0$ (very close ticks), $\nu \to 1$ and $(1-\nu) \to 0$, so we mainly use $x_{n-1}$. When $\alpha$ is large, the interpolation gives more weight to the new value $x_n$. It is a rectangular integration scheme of the exponential impulse response.

What is a scaling law in intrinsic time, and why is their existence scientifically significant? ▾

A scaling law is a power-law relation $Y \sim X^\beta$ that remains valid over several orders of magnitude. In the context of intrinsic time, we observe relations like $\langle N_\text{tick}\rangle \sim (\delta/c)^\beta$ between threshold δ and the average number of ticks in a DC, with β ≈ 2 for FX markets.

Their significance is multiple: (1) Universality — the same laws apply to 13 different currency pairs with similar exponents, suggesting a common underlying mechanism. (2) Fractal structure — power laws across orders of magnitude indicate self-similarity of the price process (as in fractals), compatible with multi-fractal process theory. (3) Practical utility — these laws allow model calibration: knowing β and c, we can predict statistical properties at different temporal resolutions. (4) Distinction vs. random walk — a Gaussian random walk also produces scaling laws, but with different exponents from real markets, revealing non-trivial microstructure properties.

Classic Pitfalls

⚠
Confusing "small α" with "high reactivity". It's the opposite! A small α gives strong smoothing and a long lag. A large α (close to 1) gives a very reactive EMA that closely follows the signal. Remember: large α → short lag, small α → long lag.
⚠
Believing that SMA(n) = EMA with α = 2/(n+1). No! This formula only equalizes the average age of the two filters. Their weight distributions are fundamentally different (uniform vs. exponential). They do not produce the same values on the same series.
⚠
Thinking that time lag is a flaw that can be eliminated. No — it is an inevitable consequence of causality. Any causal filter (based on the past) that smooths introduces a lag. The only way to eliminate lag is to use the future (non-causal filters), impossible in real-time trading.
⚠
Confusing DC with total movement (TM). The DC is only the portion of the movement that "confirms" the direction change (exactly δ% from the extremum). The TM includes DC plus the Overshoot. TM ≠ DC, and the OS can be several times larger than the DC.
⚠
Forgetting EMA initial conditions. The EMA needs a "warm-up" period before producing stable values. Using the EMA from the very first tick can give very biased results. In practice, we wait several multiples of $1/\alpha$ steps, or initialize with an SMA.
⚠
Thinking that intrinsic time is independent of δ. In reality, intrinsic time is parametric in δ — different δs give different "intrinsic times". A smaller δ creates a finer temporal resolution (more DCs), a larger δ creates a coarser resolution. It is a family of temporal representations, not a unique one.
⚠
Believing that intrinsic time predicts prices. No! The intrinsic time framework is descriptive, not predictive in itself. The scaling laws describe the historical statistical properties of the market. To make it a trading tool, an additional assumption about the stationarity of these laws is needed.

Final Revision Sheet

Real Data

  • Tick data: irregular, noisy, spread
  • Bid/Ask/Mid: 3 series, not one
  • Discretization: 99.9% of cases, info loss
  • Interpolation: rare, strong assumptions
  • Activity: very heterogeneous in time

SMA — keys

  • Weights: uniform $1/n$
  • Update: $\text{SMA}(t+1) = \text{SMA}(t) - X_{t-n+1}/n + X_{t+1}/n$
  • Memory: last $n$ values
  • Lag: $(n-1)/2$ — inevitable
  • Flaw: equally weighted

WMA — keys

  • Weights: linear $k/[n(n+1)/2]$
  • Weight sum: $n(n+1)/2$
  • Update: requires Total(t)
  • Advantage: more recent → heavier
  • Flaw: complex update, O(n) memory

EMA — keys ⭐

  • Recurrence: $\alpha X_t + (1-\alpha)\text{EMA}(t-1)$
  • α from SMA: $\alpha = 2/(n+1)$
  • α from impact: $\alpha = 1 - e^{\ln(\epsilon)/k}$
  • Memory: O(1) ← major advantage
  • Weights: $\alpha(1-\alpha)^k$ exponential

Asynchronous EMA

  • α: $(t_n-t_{n-1})/\tau$
  • μ: $e^{-\alpha}$ (forgetting factor)
  • ν: $(1-\mu)/\alpha$ (interpolation)
  • Long silence: μ→0, EMA "forgets"
  • Extension: iterated EMA, SMA of EMAs

Intrinsic Time — keys ⭐

  • DC: reversal of δ% from extremum
  • OS: continuation after DC until next extremum
  • TM = DC + OS: total movement
  • IT advances: only at DC events
  • Small δ: fine time, more DCs (volatility)
  • Long OS: low liquidity

DC Algorithm

  • State: mode (UP/DOWN) + $x_\text{ext}$
  • DC DOWN: $(x_\text{ext}-x)/x_\text{ext} \geq \delta$ if mode=UP
  • DC UP: $(x-x_\text{ext})/x_\text{ext} \geq \delta$ if mode=DOWN
  • Complexity: O(1) per tick
  • Init: arbitrary mode, converges quickly

Scaling Laws

  • Form: $Y \sim X^\beta$ (power law)
  • $\langle\Delta t_\text{dc}\rangle$: $\sim (\delta/c)^\beta$
  • Tick count: $\langle N\rangle \sim (\delta/c)^\beta$
  • 12 laws: validated on 13 FX pairs
  • Signifies: fractal market structure
The essentials in one sentence

Real markets produce noisy and irregular data that we smooth with moving averages (SMA → WMA → EMA, each more sophisticated and reactive); to go further, we can abandon physical time in favor of intrinsic time, defined by directional changes, which captures the natural market dynamics and obeys universal scaling laws relating volatility, liquidity, and microstructure.
