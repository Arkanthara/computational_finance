01

Executive Summary (30 seconds)

▶ The Essentials

Algorithmic execution consists of breaking a large order into slices, in order to minimize transaction costs, particularly market impact. Different algorithms (TWAP, VWAP, POV, IS, AS, MC, PI, LIQ, MI) target different objectives (time, volume, price, liquidity). The choice is made on the efficient frontier of cost/risk, guided by the risk aversion parameter $\lambda = \frac{\text{cost}}{\text{risk}}$.

02

Model Oral Response

Algorithmic execution addresses a fundamental problem in market finance: how to buy or sell a large quantity of assets without moving the market against oneself? This phenomenon is called market impact — it is the price change caused by one's own trading activity. It decomposes into a temporary impact, which reverts after the transaction, and a permanent impact, which reflects durable information incorporated by the market.

To minimize this impact, the order is split into slices and executed according to different strategies. The simplest algorithm is TWAP (Time Weighted Average Price): the order is divided into equal slices over a fixed time horizon, regardless of the market. It is simple, but predictable and insensitive to market conditions.

VWAP (Volume Weighted Average Price) improves on this by proportioning slices to intraday historical volume — we trade more when the market is active. POV (Percentage Of Volume) goes further by reacting to real-time volume: $w_i = \alpha \cdot v_i$.

More sophisticated algorithms like IS (Implementation Shortfall) seek to minimize the gap between the decision price and the execution price, dynamically balancing market impact and timing risk. AS (Adaptive Shortfall) adds price trend reactivity via two behaviors: AIM (more aggressive when favorable) and PIM (more aggressive against an adverse trend).

All these algorithms can be positioned on an efficient frontier in the cost/risk space. For a given risk level, the optimal algorithm is the one on the frontier. The right algorithm is chosen via the risk aversion parameter $\lambda$ which geometrically corresponds to the slope of the tangent line to the frontier from the origin.

Market Players & Market Impact

Market Ecosystem

Orders in financial markets pass through a chain of actors. The buy-side (fund managers, institutional investors) generates decisions; the sell-side (brokers) facilitates execution.

Order Flow — Buy-side → Market (from B. Johnson [8])

Market Impact: Decomposition

Market impact is the price modification induced by the mere presence of the order. It is the central problem of execution: one must not disturb the market with one's own activity.

⚠ Execution Paradox

The faster we execute → the greater the price impact (high cost, low timing risk).<
The slower we execute → the smaller the impact, but the greater the timing risk (the price moves against us in the meantime).

Market Impact Decomposition — Temporary vs Permanent Impact
Fundamental Decomposition
$$\text{Market Impact} = \underbrace{\text{Temporary Impact}}_{\text{reverts}} + \underbrace{\text{Permanent Impact}}_{\text{persists}}$$

05

TWAP — Time Weighted Average Price

TWAP

Fundamental Principle

TWAP uniformly divides the total order $w$ into equal slices over a time horizon $[t_0, t_1]$ with step $\Delta t$. The algorithm is entirely independent of price and volume — it trades on the clock.

Number of slices: $n = \frac{t_1 - t_0}{\Delta t} + 1$, each slice of size $\frac{w}{n}$.

Size of each TWAP Slice
$$w_i = \frac{w}{n} = \frac{w}{\frac{t_1 - t_0}{\Delta t} + 1}$$
Numerical Example
$w = 10\,000$ shares, $t_0 = \text{now}$, $t_1 = t_0 + 1\text{h}$, $\Delta t = 15\text{min}$ → $n = 4 + 1 = 5$ slices of 2,000 shares each, executed at $t_0, t_0+15', t_0+30', t_0+45', t_0+60'$.

TWAP Variants

  • Aggressive: trade more at the beginning of the period (front-loaded) — assumes mean reversion
  • Passive: trade more at the end of the period (back-loaded) — lets time pass
  • Randomization: add noise to sizes ($w_i + \epsilon$) and times ($t_i + \delta$) to reduce predictability
  • Price-adaptive: dynamic adjustment according to price (then approaches PI)

Strengths & Limits

✓ STRENGTHS

  • Very simple to implement
  • Easy to explain to clients
  • Guarantees 100% execution

✗ LIMITS

  • Highly predictable
  • Ignores price and volume
  • Not on the efficient frontier
TWAP Completion Curves — Neutral, Aggressive, Passive
06

VWAP — Volume Weighted Average Price

VWAP

VWAP Benchmark

VWAP is both a benchmark and an execution algorithm. As a benchmark, it is the volume-weighted average price over a period — a simple and standard measure of execution quality. Buying below VWAP = good trade. Selling above = good trade.

VWAP Benchmark Formula Benchmark
$$\text{VWAP} = \frac{\sum_i p_i \, v_i}{\sum_i v_i}$$
Variables

$i$ = index of each transaction between $t_0$ and $t_1$<
$p_i$ = price of transaction $i$<
$v_i$ = volume of transaction $i$

Intuition

VWAP gives more weight to large-volume transactions. It reflects the actual market conditions of the day. Problem: $\sum_i v_i$ is unknown before the end of the period!

Practical Problem

We only know $\sum_i v_i$ at the end of the day. Solution: use historical data to estimate the volume profile.

Numerical Example

$w = 10\,000$, $H = 50\,000$ (historical daily volume)
At 8:00 : $h_i / H = 5.2\%$ → trade $520$ shares
At 8:15 : $h_i / H = 5.0\%$ → trade $500$ shares

Typical Intraday Volume Profile (VWAP Seasonality)
Key Insight: The VWAP Paradox

The denominator $\sum_i v_i$ is only known at the end of the day. To execute a VWAP order during the day, we must rely on historical data. If the day's volume deviates significantly from the historical pattern, the algorithm will drift from the actual VWAP. Variations include adjustments based on current market conditions to reduce this dependence.

07

POV — Percentage Of Volume

POV

Trading Proportional to Real-time Volume

POV improves on VWAP by using observed real-time volume (rather than historical). For each interval $i$ of duration $\Delta t$, the traded slice is proportional to the observed volume $v_i$.

POV Formulas Real-time Volume
$$w_i = \alpha \cdot v_i$$
$$\tilde{\alpha} = \frac{\alpha}{1 - \alpha} \quad \text{(adjustment if excluding own orders)}$$
Parameter $\alpha$

Participation rate in total volume.
$1\%$ → discreet participation
$10\%$ → average participation
$20\%$ → significant participation

Correction $\tilde{\alpha}$

If $\alpha = 5\%$:
$\tilde{\alpha} = \frac{0.05}{1-0.05} = 5.26\%$<

We exclude our own trades from volume $v_i$ because we must not count twice.

Example

$w = 10\,000$, $\alpha = 5\%$<
8:15 : $v_i = 3000$ → $w_i = 150$<
8:30 : $v_i = 2700$ → $w_i = 135$<
→ The order finishes when $\sum w_i = 10\,000$ or at $t_1$.

VWAP vs POV Difference

VWAP: planning based on historical volume, executes 100% of $w$.
POV: planning based on real-time volume, may not execute 100% of $w$ if $t_1$ is unspecified.

Warning: POV Predictability

Like TWAP, POV is predictable — market makers can anticipate your orders. Solution: randomize time intervals $\Delta t$ and integrate volume forecasts (volume forecasting) to anticipate deviations. A price-adaptive version adjusts $\alpha$ according to the current price vs benchmark.

08

MI, IS, AS — Sophisticated Algorithms

MI

MI — Minimal Impact

Principle

Logical progression from VWAP/POV — exclusive focus on minimizing market impact. Uses dark pools and hidden order types (hidden orders) to reduce impact risk. Dark pools allow execution at the midprice without transparency.

Completion Risk

A dark pool may fail to execute the order if liquidity is insufficient. MI is therefore coupled with a passive VWAP/POV algorithm (e.g. 20% of the remainder) to guarantee a minimum execution.

Variations
  • Execution probability estimation in the dark pool
  • Impact cost model for benchmarking
Position on the Efficient Frontier

High risk of execution price variation, low costs of impact. Located at the bottom right of the frontier.

IS

IS — Implementation Shortfall

Objective

Minimize the gap between the decision price and the execution price. This is the real cost of execution — "what we pay extra compared to if we could have traded instantly at the decision price".

Shortfall Formula

IS = (Average Execution Price − Decision Price) × Quantity
The objective is to find the optimal time horizon and the optimal participation rate.

IS Tradeoff: Impact vs Timing Risk
Typical IS Inputs
Short-term volatility · Implied volatility (options) · Asset type · Investor risk aversion · Liquidity conditions
AS

AS — Adaptive Shortfall

Derived from IS with adaptive behavior to price movements. Two opposite behaviors depending on the assumption about trends:

AIM — Aggressive In-the-Money

Strategy: more aggressive when the price is favorable, less when unfavorable.
Hypothesis: trends are short-lived and will reverse. So we take advantage of the good price now before it disappears.

→ Counter-trend trading

PIM — Passive In-the-Money

Strategy: more passive when favorable, more aggressive when unfavorable.
Hypothesis: trends persist. If the price moves in the wrong direction, trade aggressively now to avoid future losses.

→ Trend-following

09

MC, PI, LIQ — Opportunistic Algorithms

MC

Market Close

Targets the closing price, widely used as a benchmark. Starting too early → significant timing risk. Too late → high market impact. The optimal horizon is estimated from the asset volatility and closing volume. Can be adapted to target any time (e.g. noon). Frontier position: top-left (low risk, high cost)

PI

Price Inline

Adapts trading activity to the market price relative to a benchmark. Two modes analogous to AS:
A: more aggressive when the price is favorable (counter-trend) — the price will reverse.
P: less aggressive when favorable (trend-following) — the trend will continue. Extension: use prices of correlated assets

LIQ

Liquidity Driven

Trades only when liquidity is available AND the price is favorable. Low spread = cheaper transaction. Difficulty: markets are fragmented (multiple venues), hidden orders make evaluation complex. Stylised facts and historical data feed liquidity models. Beware of illiquid assets: a small order can move the price

PORTFOLIO

Portfolio Trading

We rarely trade a single asset. We must execute simultaneously numerous orders to maintain diversification and alignment with the benchmark. Portfolio risk measures can be integrated into the execution algorithm. Alternative: hand the entire portfolio to a broker (but this reveals your positions!). "Algos of algos" may be necessary

Efficient Frontier

Just as Modern Portfolio Theory (Markowitz) defines an efficient frontier in the return/risk space, we can define an efficient frontier of execution algorithms in the cost/risk space.

Definition

An algorithm is on the efficient frontier (efficient frontier) if there exists no algorithm with the same risk but lower cost, or the same cost but lower risk. For a given risk, we select the algorithm with minimal cost.

Efficient Frontier of Execution Algorithms

Interpretation of Positions

AlgoRiskCostJustification
MC Very lowVery high The closing price is known (low variability) but reaching it is costly
IS LowHigh Seeks to execute quickly → reduces timing risk but increases impact
VWAP/POV MediumMedium Follows the market, balanced compromise
MI Very highVery low Dark pools → low impact but high uncertainty on obtained price
TWAP ⚠ NOT on the efficient frontier Dominated algorithm: there is always a more efficient algo for the same risk
12

Risk Aversion $\lambda$

Once on the efficient frontier, how to choose the optimal algorithm? The answer: via the risk aversion (risk aversion) parameter $\lambda$.

Risk Aversion λ Selection Criterion
$$\lambda = \frac{\text{cost}}{\text{risk}}$$
High λ (risk-averse)

The investor is very sensitive to risk. They accept to pay more to reduce risk. Optimal algo: towards MC, IS (top-left of frontier).

Low λ (risk-tolerant)

The investor tolerates high risk to minimize costs. Optimal algo: towards MI, LIQ (bottom-right of frontier).

Geometric Interpretation

$\lambda$ is the slope of the tangent line to the frontier from the origin. The tangency point is the optimal algorithm. $\lambda \uparrow$ → steeper tangent → algo towards top-left.

Portfolio Analogy

Exactly analogous to Markowitz: the Sharpe ratio = return/risk. Here λ = cost/risk, and the λ line plays the role of the capital market line.

Algorithm Selection via λ — Geometry of the Efficient Frontier
Key Geometric Insight

$\lambda$ is the slope of the line from the origin (0, 0) tangent to the frontier. For a given $\lambda$, the algorithm to select is the point of tangency between this line and the efficient frontier. The larger $\lambda$ (steep slope) → algorithm towards MC (low risk, high cost). The smaller $\lambda$ (gentle slope) → algorithm towards MI (high risk, low cost).

14

Possible Exam Questions

▶ Easy Level
What is market impact and why is it a problem for executing large orders? ▶

Market impact is the price change caused by one's own trading activity. When buying a large number of shares at once, sellers see a major buyer coming and pull their offers or raise their prices. Result: we pay more than the initial price.

It decomposes into temporary impact (the market returns to its level once the order is done — rebound phenomenon) and permanent impact (the market incorporates the underlying information of the order — e.g. if the order is informative, the price does not revert).

Solution: split the order into small slices executed progressively → this is the objective of algorithmic execution.

Explain TWAP. What are its advantages and disadvantages? Why is it not on the efficient frontier? ▶

TWAP divides the total order $w$ into $n = (t_1-t_0)/\Delta t + 1$ equal slices of size $w/n$, executed at regular intervals. It is entirely independent of price and volume.

Advantages: extremely simple, guaranteed to execute 100% of the order, easy to explain and audit.
Disadvantages: highly predictable (exploitable by adversarial traders), ignores market information (volume, price), may trade at times of low or high liquidity without adapting.

Not on the efficient frontier: for a risk level equivalent to TWAP, algorithms like VWAP or POV obtain a better execution price by adapting to volume. TWAP is therefore dominated — there is always a better algorithm for the same risk.

What is the VWAP benchmark formula and why can't we use it directly in real time? ▶

$\text{VWAP} = \frac{\sum_i p_i v_i}{\sum_i v_i}$ where $i$ indexes transactions in $[t_0, t_1]$, $p_i$ the price and $v_i$ the volume.

The problem is that $\sum_i v_i$ (total daily volume) is only known at the end of the day. We cannot therefore calculate the exact VWAP in real time. Solution: use the historical volume profile to estimate the intraday volume distribution. If actual volume deviates from the historical profile, the VWAP algorithm will drift from the actual VWAP.

▶ Intermediate Level
Explain the difference between AIM and PIM in the AS algorithm. When to use one rather than the other? ▶

AS is an adaptive variant of IS that reacts to price movements. Two opposite philosophies:

AIM (Aggressive In-the-Money): we trade more aggressively when the price is favorable (low for a buy, high for a sell) and less when it is unfavorable. Underlying hypothesis: trends are short-lived and will reverse (mean-reversion). So we take advantage of the good price quickly before it disappears.

PIM (Passive In-the-Money): the opposite — we are passive when the price is favorable and aggressive when unfavorable. Hypothesis: the trend will persist. If the price moves in the wrong direction, better to trade aggressively now before it gets worse.

When to use which? AIM in markets with strong mean-reversion (e.g. intraday). PIM in trending markets (e.g. momentum). The choice depends on the market regime analysis.

Describe the efficient frontier of execution algorithms. How are MC and MI positioned on this frontier? What is λ? ▶

The efficient frontier in the (risk, cost) space identifies, for each risk level, the algorithm with minimal cost. We must never choose an algorithm off the frontier (dominated).

MC (Market Close): located at the top-left — very low risk (we target a known price: the close) but very high cost (reaching that price precisely is costly, and arriving too early exposes to timing risk).

MI (Minimal Impact): at the bottom-right — very low impact costs (dark pools, passive orders) but very high risk (we do not control the obtained price nor the execution time).

$\lambda = \text{cost}/\text{risk}$ is the investor's risk aversion parameter. Geometrically, $\lambda$ is the slope of the line from the origin tangent to the frontier. The tangency point is the optimal algorithm. A high $\lambda$ → very risk-averse investor → algorithm towards MC.

Why does POV include a correction $\tilde{\alpha} = \alpha/(1-\alpha)$? Give a numerical example. ▶

In POV, $\alpha$ represents the fraction of observed volume $v_i$ that we wish to trade. However, if we have already executed a fraction $\alpha$ of volume $v_i$, this volume $v_i$ includes our own trades. To avoid "counting twice", we calculate the volume excluding our own orders: $v_i^{\text{market}} = v_i - w_i = v_i(1-\alpha)$. The corrected participation rate relative to market volume is therefore:

$$\tilde{\alpha} = \frac{w_i}{v_i^{\text{market}}} = \frac{\alpha \cdot v_i}{v_i(1-\alpha)} = \frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}$$

Example: $\alpha = 5\%$ → $\tilde{\alpha} = 0.05/0.95 \approx 5.26\%$. $\alpha = 20\%$ → $\tilde{\alpha} = 0.20/0.80 = 25\%$ — the correction becomes significant for high $\alpha$.

▶ Hard Level
Compare VWAP and POV on their logic, assumptions, and behaviors in case of an intraday liquidity shock. ▶

VWAP: a priori planning based on historical volume profile. Executes a fixed plan from the start: at 8:00 we know we will trade 5.2% of the total, at 8:15: 5.0%, etc. The plan is deterministic. Assumption: the day's volume will follow the historical pattern.

POV: a posteriori decision at each interval: we observe $v_i$ and trade $\alpha \cdot v_i$. Purely reactive. Assumption: future volume is unknown, we react to what happens.

Liquidity shock:

  • VWAP: if actual volume deviates massively from the historical profile (e.g. surprise announcement), the algorithm does not adapt → potentially large gap between actual and target VWAP. It must be corrected manually or integrate real-time adjustments.
  • POV: adapts automatically — if volume drops, $w_i$ drops too. Drawback: the total order may not be executed on time if overall volume is low.

In practice: VWAP is preferred for stable markets with good volume profile predictability. POV is preferred for more volatile markets or atypical assets.

Discuss the portfolio trading problem and how it differs from executing a single order. ▶

Single order: we optimize the execution of a single asset → well-defined impact/timing tradeoff.

Portfolio trading: we must execute simultaneously $N$ orders to maintain diversification and alignment with the benchmark. The additional problems are:

  • Cross-asset correlations: executing one asset affects the others (cross-impact effects). Portfolio risk measures must be integrated into the execution algorithm.
  • Progressive misalignment: if we execute sequentially, the portfolio is temporarily off-benchmark.
  • Information problem: if we hand the entire portfolio to a broker, we reveal our positions — risk of front-running.
  • Algorithmic complexity: "algos of algos" are necessary — each asset has its own execution algorithm, coordinated by a higher-level algorithm.

Classic Pitfalls

⚠
Confusing VWAP as benchmark and VWAP as algorithm. VWAP is first a reference price $\sum p_i v_i / \sum v_i$. The VWAP algorithm seeks to execute near this benchmark using historical data. These are two distinct things.
⚠
Thinking TWAP is "good" because it is simple. TWAP is technically off the efficient frontier — it is dominated. For an equivalent risk level, VWAP or POV offer better results. Its only virtue is operational simplicity.
⚠
Forgetting that $\sum_i v_i$ is unknown in real time for VWAP. A classic trap is to describe the VWAP algorithm as if it used the day's actual volume. No — it uses historical volume. Knowledge of the total volume only arrives at the end of the day.
⚠
Confusing AIM and PIM (difficult to memorize). AIM = Aggressive In-the-Money = aggressive when favorable → presupposes mean reversion. PIM = Passive In-the-Money = passive when favorable → presupposes trend persistence. Mnemonic trick: "AIM" aims (at an opportunity that will disappear); "PIM" patiently waits (the trend continues).
⚠
Misinterpreting λ on the efficient frontier. High λ means strong risk aversion → we pay more (high cost) to risk less. This is counter-intuitive compared to a risk premium: here "risk-averse" costs more, because we execute faster and more surely (e.g. MC).
⚠
Neglecting delay cost (24%!). Many focus on market impact (6%) and forget that delay cost (24%) is far more important. The time between the investment decision and broker receipt can represent a large fraction of total cost.
⚠
Confusing temporary and permanent impact. Temporary impact reverts once the order is executed (mechanical price rebound). Permanent impact reflects information incorporated by the market and does not revert. The decomposition is fundamental for modeling execution costs.

Final Revision Sheet

Fundamental Concepts

  • Market Impact = price changes because of one's own order
  • Temporary = rebound after trade (mechanical)
  • Permanent = info incorporated, persists
  • Delay cost = between decision and broker (24%!)
  • Timing risk = cost variability (40%!)
  • Opportunity cost = unexecuted order

Key Formulas

  • VWAP $= \sum p_i v_i / \sum v_i$

  • PIM = passive if favorable
