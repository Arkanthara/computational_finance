Portfolio Construction

Modern Portfolio Theory, Efficient Frontier, Capital Market Line — from Markowitz to numerical optimization.

MPT Efficient Frontier Optimization CML · Sharpe Markowitz 1952
00 Setting the Scene 😂
Imagine you have 100 CHF. You can put everything on Tesla stock (maximum thrill!), everything in cash under your mattress (maximum safety and boredom), or think about a smart allocation. Spoiler: neither 100% Tesla nor the mattress will make Markowitz proud. But if you mix the two judiciously, you might sleep soundly AND get richer. That's exactly what this chapter will teach you.
"Diversification is the only free lunch in finance." — Harry Markowitz, Nobel Prize 1990, who clearly had a passion for all-you-can-eat buffets.

This chapter addresses one of the fundamental problems in finance: how to intelligently allocate capital across different assets? The answer, surprisingly simple in its mathematical formulation, is that combining imperfectly correlated assets allows you to reduce risk without sacrificing return — or increase return without increasing risk. That's the magic of diversification, formalized by Markowitz in 1952.

01 Executive Summary
⏱ 30 seconds flat

The Modern Portfolio Theory (MPT), proposed by Markowitz in 1952, formalizes how to combine assets to maximize expected return for a given level of risk (or minimize risk for a target return). The key is diversification: imperfectly correlated assets partially offset each other, reducing the overall portfolio variance. The set of optimal portfolios forms the Efficient Frontier. Adding a risk-free asset yields the Capital Market Line (CML), which identifies the optimal tangency portfolio.

Problem

Optimal Allocation

How to allocate capital across $N$ assets to be efficient in the (risk, return) space?

Central Tool

Covariance Matrix

Encodes all relationships between assets. Together with expected returns, it is sufficient for all calculations.

Key Concept

Diversification

If $\rho < 1$, combining two assets reduces risk relative to the weighted average.

Result

Efficient Frontier

Curve of dominant portfolios — no one can do better in risk/return simultaneously.

02 Model Oral Exam Answer

Portfolio construction aims to create an investment vehicle more robust than any single asset. The theoretical foundation is Modern Portfolio Theory by Markowitz, proposed in 1952 and awarded the Nobel Prize in Economics in 1990.

The central idea is diversification: by combining assets whose returns are not perfectly correlated, one can reduce overall risk without necessarily sacrificing return. Mathematically, a portfolio's variance depends not only on the individual variances of the assets, but also on the covariances between them. If two assets move in opposite directions, their fluctuations partially cancel out.

For a portfolio of $N$ assets with weights $\mathbf{w}$, the expected return is $\mathbf{w}^T \boldsymbol{\mu}$ and the variance is $\mathbf{w}^T \mathbf{C} \mathbf{w}$, where $\mathbf{C}$ is the covariance matrix. We then seek the weights that minimize risk for a target return, subject to the constraint $\sum w_i = 1$. The set of these optimal solutions forms the Efficient Frontier in the (volatility, return) space.

In practice, this frontier can be computed by three approaches: Monte Carlo (brute force, intuitive but costly), closed-form via Lagrange multipliers (analytical, but inflexible), or numerical optimization (the standard approach, flexible and efficient). Optimization easily accommodates realistic constraints such as bounds on weights.

Finally, by introducing a risk-free asset — typically a government bond — we can extend the efficient frontier into a straight line in the return/risk space: the Capital Market Line. The tangency point between the CML and the efficient frontier is the optimal market portfolio, and the return/risk ratio of the CML is the famous Sharpe Ratio.

03 In-Depth Explanation

3.1 What is a Portfolio?

A portfolio is a collection of potentially different types of assets (stocks, bonds, real estate, commodities, etc.) characterized by a weight vector $\mathbf{w} = (w_1, \ldots, w_N)^T$ with $\sum_{i=1}^N w_i = 1$.

💡 Intuition on Weights

A weight $w_i > 0$ means a long position (you own the asset). A weight $w_i < 0$ means a short position (short selling — you sell an asset you do not own). In a "long-only" portfolio, we impose $w_i \in [0,1]$. Unconstrained optimization may yield negative weights (leverage and short-selling).

3.2 Return and Variance — The Essentials

The robustness of the portfolio depends on the right choice of assets and their weightings. We seek to characterize each asset by its expected return $E[R_i]$ and its variance $\text{Var}(R_i) = \sigma_i^2$.

Estimating Expected Return

Practitioners often say that "expected returns are more an art than a science." Three approaches exist:

ApproachFormula / MethodHorizonAdvantageLimitation
Historical (SMA) $E[R_i] = \frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^T R_i(t)$ Medium Simple, objective Assumes the past repeats itself
Fundamental Dividends, coupons, macro Long term Anchored in the real economy Difficult to quantify
Opportunistic Macro + market views Short term Incorporates current information Subjective, risk of bias

3.3 Diversification — The Heart of the Matter

The benefit of diversification stems directly from the portfolio variance formula:

Variance of an N-Asset Portfolio
$$\sigma_P^2 = \text{Var}(R_P) = \sum_{i=1}^N \sum_{j=1}^N w_i w_j \text{Cov}(R_i, R_j) = \mathbf{w}^T \mathbf{C} \mathbf{w}$$

Expanding for 2 assets with $\text{Cov}(R_i, R_j) = \rho_{ij}\sigma_i\sigma_j$:

Variance of a 2-Asset Portfolio
$$\sigma_P^2 = w_1^2\sigma_1^2 + w_2^2\sigma_2^2 + 2w_1 w_2 \rho_{12} \sigma_1 \sigma_2$$
⚡ The Moment of Truth — Correlation Effect

The third term $2w_1 w_2 \rho_{12} \sigma_1 \sigma_2$ is the key:
• If $\rho_{12} = 1$ → maximum variance (no diversification benefit)
• If $\rho_{12} = 0$ → partial reduction (independent assets)
• If $\rho_{12} = -1$ → maximum reduction (risk cancellation possible!)<
Conclusion: the lower the correlation, the more effective the diversification.

3.4 The Efficient Frontier

The Efficient Frontier is the set of portfolios that, for a given level of risk $\sigma_P$, maximize the expected return $E[R_P]$ — or equivalently, for a target return, minimize risk.

Dominance: Portfolio A dominates Portfolio C if A offers the same return with less risk, or more return with the same risk. Any portfolio below the frontier is called inefficient.

📐 Mathematical Shape

The frontier is a hyperbola in the $(\sigma_P, E[R_P])$ space. It has a global minimum variance point (Global Minimum Variance Portfolio, GMVP) at its leftmost end. Only the upper portion (above the GMVP) constitutes the Markowitz efficient frontier.

05 Important Formulas
Portfolio Return Linearity of expectation
$$E[R_P] = \sum_{i=1}^N w_i E[R_i] = \mathbf{w}^T \boldsymbol{\mu}$$
Variables

$w_i$ : weight of asset $i$<
$E[R_i]$ : expected return of asset $i$<
$\boldsymbol{\mu}$ : vector of expected returns

Intuition

The portfolio return is a weighted average of the asset returns. This is a simple property of the linearity of expectation. No magic here — we do not create return by combining assets.

Numerical example

$w = (0.5, 0.5)$, $E[R_1]=0.1$, $E[R_2]=0.2$<
→ $E[R_P] = 0.5 \times 0.1 + 0.5 \times 0.2 = 0.15$

Key pedagogical point

Diversification does not create return. It reduces risk. This is the fundamental difference with variance.

Portfolio Variance Quadratic form
$$\sigma_P^2 = \mathbf{w}^T \mathbf{C} \mathbf{w} = \sum_{i=1}^N \sum_{j=1}^N w_i w_j \sigma_{ij}$$
Variables

$\mathbf{C}$ : covariance matrix ($N \times N$)
$\sigma_{ij} = \text{Cov}(R_i, R_j) = \rho_{ij}\sigma_i\sigma_j$<
$\sigma_{ii} = \sigma_i^2$ (own variance)

Magic intuition

The cross term $2w_i w_j \rho_{ij} \sigma_i \sigma_j$ is what enables risk reduction. If $\rho_{ij} < 1$, the portfolio variance is lower than the weighted average of individual variances!

2-asset case

$$\sigma_P^2 = w_1^2\sigma_1^2 + 2w_1w_2\rho_{12}\sigma_1\sigma_2 + w_2^2\sigma_2^2$$

Data requirement

To be statistically significant, one needs at least $N^2/2$ data points. For $N=50$ assets, this means $\geq 1250$ observations!

Pearson Correlation Normalized
$$\rho_{ij} = \frac{\text{Cov}(R_i, R_j)}{\sigma_i \sigma_j} \in [-1, 1]$$
Special cases

• $\rho_{ii} = 1$ always
• $\rho_{ij} = 1$ → straight line in (σ,E[R]) space
• $\rho_{ij} = -1$ → risk cancellable if $w_1\sigma_1 = w_2\sigma_2$<
• $\rho_{ij} = 0$ → independent assets

Problem in crisis

During market crises (2008, COVID), correlations drastically increase (trend towards 1), reducing the effectiveness of diversification exactly when it is most needed.

Markowitz Closed-Form Solution Lagrange multipliers
$$a = \mathbf{1}^T \mathbf{C}^{-1} \mathbf{1}, \quad b = \mathbf{1}^T \mathbf{C}^{-1} \boldsymbol{\mu}, \quad c = \boldsymbol{\mu}^T \mathbf{C}^{-1} \boldsymbol{\mu}, \quad d = ac - b^2$$ $$\lambda_1 = \frac{c - b\mu_P}{d}, \quad \lambda_2 = \frac{a\mu_P - b}{d}$$ $$\mathbf{w}^* = \mathbf{C}^{-1}(\lambda_1 \mathbf{1} + \lambda_2 \boldsymbol{\mu}), \quad \sigma_P^{*2} = \lambda_1 + \lambda_2 \mu_P$$
Single constraint

This formula only constrains $\sum w_i = 1$. It can therefore yield negative weights (short selling) or weights above 1 (leverage). For additional constraints, numerical optimization is required.

Key operation

Inverting $\mathbf{C}^{-1}$ is costly ($O(N^3)$) and can be numerically unstable if $\mathbf{C}$ is near-singular (highly correlated assets). Regularization techniques (shrinkage, Ledoit-Wolf) are often used in practice.

Numerical Optimization Problem Standard method
$$\min_{\mathbf{w}} \;\; \mathbf{w}^T \mathbf{C} \mathbf{w} - q \, \mathbf{w}^T \boldsymbol{\mu}$$ $$\text{s.t.} \;\; \mathbf{1}^T \mathbf{w} = 1, \quad 0 \leq w_i \leq 1 \;\; \forall i$$
Parameter $q$

$q \in [0, +\infty)$ is the risk tolerance parameter.<
• $q=0$ → minimum variance portfolio
• $q \to \infty$ → maximum return portfolio
By varying $q$, we trace the efficient frontier.

Nature of the problem

This is a Quadratic Programming (QP) problem (quadratic objective, linear constraints). If $\mathbf{C}$ is positive definite, the problem is convex → guarantee of a global minimum. Solvers: scipy.optimize, CVXPY, Gurobi.

Capital Market Line & Sharpe Ratio Tangency portfolio
$$E[R_{P1}] = w_{rf} r_f + (1 - w_{rf}) E[R_{P0}], \quad \sigma_{P1} = (1 - w_{rf}) \sigma_{P0}$$ $$\text{Sharpe Ratio} = SR = \frac{E[R_T] - r_f}{\sigma_T}$$
Interpretation

Combining the risk-free asset $r_f$ with a portfolio $P_0$ gives a linear relationship between risk and return of the combined portfolio. The risk-free asset is uncorrelated with any other asset ($\rho_{rf,P} = 0$ and $\sigma_{rf} = 0$).

Optimal portfolio

The tangency portfolio T is the one that maximizes the Sharpe Ratio on the efficient frontier. Every rational investor (whatever their risk/return preferences) should hold T combined with the risk-free asset.

Leverage

If $w_{rf} < 0$, the investor borrows at rate $r_f$ to invest more in T (leverage). If $w_{rf} > 1$, they invest more in the risk-free asset than in T (very conservative).

CAPM

In the CAPM, all investors hold the same portfolio T = market portfolio. The CML then becomes the market line (Capital Market Line in its most general sense).

08 Historical Perspective
1952
Harry Markowitz — Modern Portfolio Theory
Publication of "Portfolio Selection" in the Journal of Finance. Mathematical formalization of diversification, introduction of the efficient frontier. Conceptual revolution: thinking in terms of (risk, return) rather than asset by asset.
1958
James Tobin — Separation Theorem
Introduction of the risk-free asset and proof that the optimal portfolio is always a combination of the tangency portfolio and the risk-free asset (separation theorem).
1964–1966
Sharpe, Lintner, Mossin — CAPM
Capital Asset Pricing Model: extension of MPT to market equilibrium. The market portfolio is the tangency portfolio. Introduction of beta $\beta$ as a measure of systematic risk.
1990
Nobel Prize — Markowitz, Sharpe, Miller
Official recognition of their fundamental contributions to the theory of finance.
2003
Ledoit & Wolf — Shrinkage Estimator
Improvement of covariance matrix estimation for large asset universes ($N$ large, $T$ limited). Systematic regularization towards a target to improve numerical stability.
2008–2009
Financial Crisis — Limits of MPT
Correlations uniformize (all near 1) during crises, canceling the benefit of diversification. Development of robust methods: Risk Parity, Black-Litterman, CVaR.
Today
MPT + Machine Learning + Factors
Expected return estimation by ML, robust optimization, factor portfolios (value, momentum, quality), advanced regularization techniques. MPT remains the reference framework, but its inputs are modernized.
09 Connections with Modern Practices
Risk management

CVaR / Expected Shortfall

Replacement of $\sigma_P^2$ by CVaR (Conditional Value at Risk) as the risk measure in optimization. Better captures non-Gaussian distribution tails. Regulated by Basel III / Solvency II.

Strategy

Risk Parity

Alternative to MPT: allocate so that each asset contributes equally to the total portfolio risk. Does not require estimating expected returns (less uncertain). Bridgewater All Weather Fund.

Bayesian

Black-Litterman

Combines CAPM-implied expected returns with investor "views" in a Bayesian manner. Reduces the extremity of allocations (Markowitz input sensitivity problem).

ML

ML Estimation of Inputs

Use of recurrent neural networks, penalized regression (LASSO/Ridge), or factor models to better estimate $E[R_i]$ and $\mathbf{C}$. The MPT optimization remains the same.

Pension Funds

ALM — Asset Liability Management

Extension of MPT where liabilities (future benefits) are also modeled. One minimizes funding risk, not just $\sigma_P^2$. Crucial for regulated pension funds (OPP2 in Switzerland).

Robo-advisors

Automation

Wealthfront, Betterment, True Wealth (CH) all use MPT as the core of their allocation engine. Mass implementation of the Markowitz framework at low cost.

⚠ Fundamental Limits of MPT

1. Input sensitivity: small changes in $E[R_i]$ or $\mathbf{C}$ can produce large changes in $\mathbf{w}^*$ ("garbage in, garbage out" problem).<
2. Stationarity: assumes distributions remain constant over time — false in practice.
3. Normality: variance is a complete risk measure only if returns are Gaussian — they are not (fat tails, skewness).<
4. Unstable correlations: during crises, correlations converge to 1, erasing the benefit of diversification.

10 Exam Questions
Easy Level
What is diversification and why is it beneficial? ▾
Diversification consists of combining assets whose returns are not perfectly correlated ($\rho < 1$). It is beneficial because the portfolio variance $\sigma_P^2 = \sum_i \sum_j w_i w_j \sigma_{ij}$ contains cross-covariance terms which, when $\rho_{ij} < 1$, make $\sigma_P^2$ lower than the weighted average of individual variances. In other words: we reduce risk without necessarily sacrificing expected return (which is simply a weighted average of returns).
What does the covariance matrix $\mathbf{C}$ represent? ▾
$\mathbf{C}$ is an $N \times N$ symmetric positive definite matrix. Its diagonal contains the variances $\sigma_i^2$ of individual assets. Its off-diagonal elements $\sigma_{ij} = \rho_{ij}\sigma_i\sigma_j$ capture the covariances between pairs of assets. It completely encodes the linear dependence relationships between assets and is the central ingredient for computing the portfolio variance $\sigma_P^2 = \mathbf{w}^T\mathbf{C}\mathbf{w}$.
What does the efficient frontier represent? ▾
The efficient frontier is the set of portfolios that maximize the expected return $E[R_P]$ for a given level of volatility $\sigma_P$ (or equivalently, minimize $\sigma_P$ for a target $E[R_P]$). Any portfolio located below or to the right of this curve is called "inefficient" because it is dominated by at least one portfolio on the frontier (same return for less risk, or more return for the same risk). The frontier is a hyperbola in the $(\sigma_P, E[R_P])$ space.
Intermediate Level
Explain the effect of $\rho_{12} = -1$ vs $\rho_{12} = +1$ on a 2-asset portfolio. ▾
For $\sigma_P^2 = w_1^2\sigma_1^2 + w_2^2\sigma_2^2 + 2w_1w_2\rho_{12}\sigma_1\sigma_2$:

• $\rho = +1$: maximum positive cross term → $\sigma_P = w_1\sigma_1 + w_2\sigma_2$ (simple weighted average). Combining the two assets does not reduce risk. In the (σ,E[R]) space, portfolios align on a straight line connecting the two assets.

• $\rho = -1$: maximum negative cross term → $\sigma_P = |w_1\sigma_1 - w_2\sigma_2|$. If $w_1\sigma_1 = w_2\sigma_2$ (i.e., $w_1 = \sigma_2/(\sigma_1+\sigma_2)$), then $\sigma_P = 0$: total risk cancellation! In practice, a correlation of $-1$ is extremely rare and even impossible to maintain over time.
Why is numerical optimization used rather than the closed-form in practice? ▾
Numerical optimization (QP) offers several decisive advantages:

1. Flexible constraints: bounds on weights ($l_i \le w_i \le u_i$), sector constraints, regulatory constraints (OPP2), FX exposure — impossible with the closed-form.
2. Long-only: the closed-form can produce negative weights (short-selling), forbidden for many institutional investors.
3. Stability: the closed-form requires $\mathbf{C}^{-1}$, which can be numerically unstable if assets are highly correlated. Modern QP solvers handle this better.
4. Extensibility: turnover penalties, liquidity constraints — all naturally added within the optimization framework.
What is the Capital Market Line and how is it constructed? ▾
The CML is constructed by combining a risk-free asset $r_f$ (government bond) with the tangency portfolio T on the efficient frontier. Since $\sigma_{rf}=0$ and $\rho_{rf,P}=0$, the combination gives: $E[R_{P1}] = w_{rf}r_f + (1-w_{rf})E[R_T]$ and $\sigma_{P1} = (1-w_{rf})\sigma_T$. This is a linear relationship, so in the $(\sigma, E[R])$ space it is a straight line starting from $(0, r_f)$ and passing through $(σ_T, E[R_T])$. Its slope is the Sharpe Ratio = $(E[R_T] - r_f)/\sigma_T$. Every portfolio on the CML is superior (dominates) every portfolio on the efficient frontier alone (at identical $\sigma$).
Hard Level
Why can the Markowitz closed-form produce weights above 1 or negative, and what are the implications? ▾
The solution $\mathbf{w}^* = \mathbf{C}^{-1}(\lambda_1\mathbf{1} + \lambda_2\boldsymbol{\mu})$ only constrains $\sum w_i = 1$ (a single Lagrange multiplier for this constraint). There is no constraint on the individual values of the weights. Thus:

• $w_i < 0$ means short selling of asset $i$ — illegal for many investors.
• $w_i > 1$ means leverage (borrowing to invest more than capital).<

Graphical implications: the unconstrained frontier lies above the long-only frontier (higher in the E[R]/σ space), because it accesses portfolios unavailable with $w_i \in [0,1]$. By imposing capping (clipping to [0,1]), the frontier is reduced and cannot reach the high risk levels of a single asset. This is precisely why numerical optimization with bound constraints is preferable in practice.
Explain why diversification fails during crises, and what alternatives exist. ▾
During crises (2008, March 2020), a contagion phenomenon causes correlations of almost all assets to converge towards $\rho \approx 1$. This is explained by massive position liquidation (all investors sell everything at the same time), generalized panic, and margin effects. The diversification benefit (term $2w_iw_j\rho_{ij}\sigma_i\sigma_j$) disappears exactly when it is most needed — a systemic failure of MPT which assumes stable correlations.

Alternatives and palliatives:
1. CVaR Optimization: uses the distribution tail as a risk measure — more robust to shocks.
2. Risk Parity: allocates according to risk contributions rather than capital. More diversified in risk.
3. Safe haven assets: gold, CHF, JGB maintain negative correlations even in crisis (partially).<
4. Dynamic correlation models (DCC-GARCH): estimate time-varying correlations rather than assuming them constant.
5. Robust Optimization: seeks portfolios that perform well over a set of adverse scenarios.
Derive the 2-asset portfolio variance formula from the definition of covariance. ▾
$R_P = w_1 R_1 + w_2 R_2$. By definition: $\sigma_P^2 = \text{Var}(w_1R_1 + w_2R_2) = E[(w_1R_1 + w_2R_2 - E[w_1R_1+w_2R_2])^2]$<
$= E[(w_1(R_1-\mu_1) + w_2(R_2-\mu_2))^2]$<
$= E[w_1^2(R_1-\mu_1)^2 + 2w_1w_2(R_1-\mu_1)(R_2-\mu_2) + w_2^2(R_2-\mu_2)^2]$<
$= w_1^2\text{Var}(R_1) + 2w_1w_2\text{Cov}(R_1,R_2) + w_2^2\text{Var}(R_2)$<
$= w_1^2\sigma_1^2 + 2w_1w_2\rho_{12}\sigma_1\sigma_2 + w_2^2\sigma_2^2$<

In matrix notation: $\sigma_P^2 = (w_1,w_2)\begin{pmatrix}\sigma_1^2 & \sigma_{12} \\ \sigma_{12} & \sigma_2^2\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}w_1\\w_2\end{pmatrix} = \mathbf{w}^T\mathbf{C}\mathbf{w}$
11 Classic Pitfalls
⚠
Confusing "diversification increases return". No! The expected return $E[R_P] = \mathbf{w}^T\boldsymbol{\mu}$ is a simple weighted average — diversification does not create return. It reduces risk at constant return (or increases return at constant risk).
⚠
Believing that $\rho_{12} = -1$ always cancels risk. No! Cancellation is only possible if $w_1\sigma_1 = w_2\sigma_2$, i.e., $w_1 = \sigma_2/(\sigma_1+\sigma_2)$. If assets have different volatilities, a residual remains. And $\rho = -1$ is quasi-nonexistent in practice.
⚠
Confusing efficient frontier with feasible set. The feasible set (Monte Carlo cloud of points) is the set of all possible portfolios. The efficient frontier is only its upper-left envelope. All portfolios below the frontier are "inefficient" but "feasible".
⚠
Thinking that the CML is the efficient frontier. The efficient frontier is a hyperbola in (σ, E[R]). The CML is a straight line tangent to this hyperbola passing through $(0, r_f)$. The CML dominates the efficient frontier because it benefits from combination with the risk-free asset.
⚠
Ignoring the instability of $\mathbf{C}^{-1}$. If assets are highly correlated, matrix $\mathbf{C}$ can be near-singular, making $\mathbf{C}^{-1}$ numerically unstable. In practice, regularization techniques (Ledoit-Wolf, ridge) are used or eigenvalues that are too small are truncated.
⚠
Confusing the optimization parameter $q$ with the Sharpe Ratio. $q$ is a parameter controlling the risk/return balance in the objective function $\min \mathbf{w}^T\mathbf{C}\mathbf{w} - q\mathbf{w}^T\boldsymbol{\mu}$. By varying $q$ from 0 to $+\infty$, we trace the frontier. It is not directly the Sharpe Ratio, but they are conceptually linked.
⚠
Underestimating the importance of expected returns. Practitioners say "expected returns are more an art than a science". Errors in $E[R_i]$ propagate directly into the optimal weights $\mathbf{w}^*$. An "optimal" portfolio with bad expected return estimates can be very suboptimal in reality.
⚠
Forgetting that the unconstrained frontier passes ABOVE the Monte Carlo cloud. This is because it allows negative weights (short-selling) and weights above 1 (leverage), which are not generated by a random draw of weights in $[0,1]$.

📐 Formulas to Remember

  • Return : $E[R_P] = \mathbf{w}^T\boldsymbol{\mu}$
  • Variance : $\sigma_P^2 = \mathbf{w}^T\mathbf{C}\mathbf{w}$
  • 2 assets : $\sigma_P^2 = w_1^2\sigma_1^2 + w_2^2\sigma_2^2 + 2w_1w_2\rho_{12}\sigma_1\sigma_2$
  • Correlation : $\rho_{ij} = \sigma_{ij}/(\sigma_i\sigma_j)$
  • Sharpe : $SR = (E[R_T]-r_f)/\sigma_T$
  • CML : $\sigma_{P1} = (1-w_{rf})\sigma_{P0}$
  • Opt. : $\min_w \mathbf{w}^T\mathbf{C}\mathbf{w} - q\mathbf{w}^T\boldsymbol{\mu}$

⚡ Correlation Effects

  • $\rho=+1$ : $\sigma_P = w_1\sigma_1+w_2\sigma_2$ (straight line)
  • $\rho=0.5$ : $\sigma_P \approx 0.087$ (symmetric)
  • $\rho=0$ : $\sigma_P \approx 0.071$ (notable gain)
  • $\rho=-0.5$ : $\sigma_P = 0.05$
  • $\rho=-1$ : $\sigma_P = 0$ if $w_i\sigma_i = w_j\sigma_j$
  • The lower $\rho$, the more effective diversification

🔧 3 Calculation Approaches

  • Monte Carlo : random, intuitive, slow
  • Closed-form : exact, free weights, $C^{-1}$ required
  • Optimization ★ : flexible, constraints, industry standard
  • Unconstrained : EF above MC cloud
  • With constraints : EF lowered, σ_max reduced

🚨 Watch Points

  • $\rho=-1$ ≠ guaranteed risk cancellation
  • Unconstrained EF ≠ Constrained EF
  • CML ≠ Efficient frontier
  • Correlations unstable in crisis
  • $E[R_i]$ "more an art than a science"
  • $\mathbf{C}^{-1}$ can be unstable if $N$ large
✅ Final Message for the Oral Exam

Markowitz's MPT (1952) remains the reference framework for portfolio management, 70 years after its publication. Its three pillars are: (1) formalize the risk/return trade-off via variance and covariances, (2) identify the efficient frontier as the set of optimal allocations, and (3) introduce the risk-free asset to define the CML and the optimal tangency portfolio. In practice, constrained numerical optimization is the standard method, allowing incorporation of realistic regulatory and institutional constraints. The limits of MPT (input sensitivity, correlations unstable in crisis) have motivated many modern extensions — Risk Parity, Black-Litterman, CVaR — but all build upon this fundamental framework.
