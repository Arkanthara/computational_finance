Options — Basics

From the definition of a right to buy/sell all the way to the Black-Scholes model: everything you need to master to shine at the oral exam.

00Setting the Stage 😄
⚡ A little story to break the ice

A trader walks into a bar and orders a beer. The bartender says: "That'll be 5 euros."

The trader replies: "I'll sell you an option on this beer. For 0.50€, I give you the right — but not the obligation — to serve it to me within the next 10 minutes at 5€. If in 10 minutes the price of beer rises to 7€ at the bar next door, I exercise my option and you lose 2€. If the price stays at 5€, you keep my 0.50€."

The bartender: "…Do you just want a beer, yes or no?"

👆 This story perfectly summarizes options: a conditional right, a premium to pay, and asymmetric risk between buyer and seller.

Options are ubiquitous in modern finance: portfolio hedging, directional speculation, yield strategies. Understanding their mechanics means understanding the foundational principle of no-arbitrage pricing — the cornerstone of all quantitative finance.

01Executive Summary (30 seconds)
✦ The essentials in 3 sentences

An option is a contract giving its holder the right (not the obligation) to buy (call) or sell (put) an asset at a fixed price (strike K) until a given date (maturity T), in exchange for paying a premium.

Its price depends on six variables: the spot price $S_0$, the strike $K$, the maturity $T$, the volatility $\sigma$, the risk-free rate $r$, and dividends.

Two pricing methods dominate: the binomial tree (discrete method, universal) and the Black-Scholes model (closed-form formula, European vanilla options).

02Model Oral Answer (2–4 min)

An option is a financial instrument that gives its holder a right — and not an obligation — to buy or sell an underlying asset at a predetermined price, called the strike, at or before a maturity date. We distinguish the call, the right to buy, from the put, the right to sell. We speak of a European option if it can only be exercised at maturity, and an American option if it can be exercised at any time.

This asymmetry — the buyer has a right, the seller has an obligation — justifies the payment of a premium. The payoff of a long call at maturity is $\max(S_T - K, 0)$: we only exercise if the market is favorable. The profit is this payoff minus the premium paid.

To bound the price of an option, we use no-arbitrage arguments: if a call were trading for more than the asset itself, one could sell the call, buy the asset, and pocket the difference risk-free — an opportunity the market would immediately correct. We thus obtain: $S_0 - Ke^{-rT} \leq c \leq S_0$ and $Ke^{-rT} - S_0 \leq p \leq K$.

The put-call parity is a fundamental equilibrium relation: $c + Ke^{-rT} = p + S_0$. It states that a call plus discounted cash is equivalent to a put plus the asset. Any deviation creates an arbitrage.

To price, the binomial tree discretizes time and builds a risk-free portfolio. We determine $\Delta$ — the number of shares to hold — to immunize against price movements, then we roll back the tree by backward induction. For vanilla options, Black-Scholes provides a closed-form formula assuming the price follows a geometric Brownian motion. Implied volatility — extracted from BS by fixing all other variables — should be constant according to the model, but in practice it forms a volatility smile, revealing the limits of Gaussian assumptions.

03Definitions & Types of Options

The 4 fundamental positions

There are 4 types of positions on options, defined by the combination (call/put) × (buyer/seller):

Buyer · Long

Long Call 📈

Right to buy the asset at $K$. We pay the premium $c$. Unlimited profit if $S_T \uparrow$. Loss limited to $c$.

Seller · Short

Short Call ⚠️

Obligation to sell if exercised. We receive $c$. Theoretically unlimited loss if $S_T \uparrow\uparrow$.

Buyer · Long

Long Put 📉

Right to sell the asset at $K$. We pay $p$. Profit if $S_T \downarrow$. Loss limited to $p$.

Seller · Short

Short Put

Obligation to buy if exercised. We receive $p$. Loss limited to $K - p$ (if $S_T \to 0$).

📌 Intuition

An option is an insurance. We pay a premium to protect ourselves against an adverse market movement. Like car insurance, if nothing happens, we "lose" the premium — but that is the price of peace of mind.

06Price Bounds — No-Arbitrage

Method: arbitrage reasoning

All bounds are proven by reductio ad absurdum: if the bound is violated, we construct a risk-free portfolio that generates a positive profit — what we call an arbitrage or "free lunch". Efficient markets eliminate it immediately.

Proof: lower bound of the call

📐 Proof by portfolio comparison

We compare two portfolios at maturity $T$:

  • Portfolio A: a call $c$ + cash $Ke^{-rT}$ (invested at rate $r$)
  • Portfolio B: the asset $S$

At maturity: if $S_T > K$ → A is worth $(S_T - K) + K = S_T$ ; if $S_T \leq K$ → A is worth $K \geq S_T$. Thus $A \geq B$ always. By no-arbitrage: $c + Ke^{-rT} \geq S_0$, hence $c \geq S_0 - Ke^{-rT}$.

08Trading Strategies

Bull Spread (via calls)

Long call at $K_1$ + Short call at $K_2$ with $K_2 > K_1$. Bullish view with both gain and loss limited.

Long Butterfly Spread

Long call $K_1$ + 2 short calls $K_2$ + long call $K_3$, with $K_3 > K_2 > K_1$. A bet on low volatility: maximum profit if $S_T = K_2$.

09Option Pricing — Binomial Tree

Fundamental principle: risk elimination by hedging

The key idea: build a portfolio that has the same value whether the option goes up or down. This risk-free portfolio must earn the risk-free rate $r$.

One-period tree diagram

Calculation pipeline

Numerical example (lecture, slide 243)

📊 European Call: K = 21$, S₀ = 20$, T = 3 months, r = 12%

$u = 1.1$, $d = 1/1.1 = 0.909$

$S_T^u = 22$, $f_u = \max(22-21, 0) = 1$ — $S_T^d = 18.2$, $f_d = 0$

Risk-neutral probability: $p = \frac{e^{0.12 \times 0.25} - 0.909}{1.1 - 0.909} = 0.6357$

Call price: $f = e^{-0.12 \times 0.25}(0.6357 \times 1 + 0.3643 \times 0) = \mathbf{0.617\$}$

Hedge delta: $\Delta = \frac{1 - 0}{22 - 18.2} = \mathbf{0.2619}$ shares to buy

Extension to multiple periods

At $n$ periods, the tree has $2^n$ possible paths. For $n = 30$, this represents about $10^9$ paths — expensive in memory but recombining (nodes $ud = du$ merge), which reduces to $O(n^2)$ distinct nodes.

10Option Pricing — Black & Scholes

Model assumptions

Assumption 1

GBM

The price follows a geometric Brownian motion (log-normal).

Assumption 2

Constant volatility

$\sigma$ is constant over the life of the option.

Assumption 3

Perfect markets

No friction, continuous trading, no dividends.

Assumption 4

Constant rate

The risk-free rate $r$ is constant and known.

Black-Scholes — Call Price European Vanilla
$$c = S_0 N(d_1) - K e^{-rT} N(d_2)$$
$d_1$

$$d_1 = \frac{\ln(S_0/K) + (r + \sigma^2/2)T}{\sigma\sqrt{T}}$$

$d_2$

$$d_2 = d_1 - \sigma\sqrt{T} = \frac{\ln(S_0/K) + (r - \sigma^2/2)T}{\sigma\sqrt{T}}$$

$N(\cdot)$

CDF of the standard normal distribution: $N(x) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{-\infty}^{x} e^{-z^2/2}dz$

Intuition

$S_0 N(d_1)$: expected value of receiving $S_T$ if we exercise. $Ke^{-rT}N(d_2)$: discounted value of paying $K$, weighted by the probability of exercising.

Black-Scholes — Put Price European Vanilla
$$p = K e^{-rT} N(-d_2) - S_0 N(-d_1)$$
Link with Put-Call Parity

This formula follows directly from: $p = c - S_0 + Ke^{-rT}$ and the call formula.

Bounds verification

When $\sigma \to \infty$ : $N(d_1) \to 1$, $N(d_2) \to 0$, so $c \to S_0$ ✓. When $\sigma \to 0$ : $c \to S_0 - Ke^{-rT}$ ✓.

Visualization of Binomial → Black-Scholes convergence

🔗 Binomial ↔ Black-Scholes Link

When $n \to \infty$ in the binomial tree, with $u = e^{\sigma\sqrt{T/n}}$ and $d = e^{-\sigma\sqrt{T/n}}$, the process converges to the geometric Brownian motion of Black-Scholes. The two methods are fundamentally equivalent in the continuous limit.

11Volatility Smile

Definition of implied volatility

Implied volatility is the value of $\sigma$ that must be plugged into Black-Scholes to recover the market price of the option, all other variables being fixed. It is a numerical inversion of the BS formula.

Numerical inversion of Black-Scholes
$$\sigma_{impl} = \text{BS}^{-1}(c_{market};\, S_0, K, r, T)$$

The volatility smile

⚠️ Fundamental implication

The volatility smile invalidates the Black-Scholes assumption that $\sigma$ is constant. In practice, the tails of the distribution are thicker than the Gaussian (fat tails), and equity markets often exhibit a negative skew (fear of decline > fear of rise). The smile is therefore a mirror of the model's limits.

Implied volatility surface

In two dimensions (strike $K$ × maturity $T$), we obtain the volatility surface — a crucial tool in options trading. It encodes all the market's pricing information and allows interpolation of prices for non-quoted strikes/maturities.

12Important Formulas
Call Payoff (Long) Definition
$$\text{Payoff}_{call} = \max(S_T - K,\, 0)$$$$\text{Profit}_{call} = \max(S_T - K,\, 0) - c$$
Variables

$S_T$ : price at maturity, $K$ : strike, $c$ : premium paid

Intuition

We only exercise if $S_T > K$. Max loss is the premium $c$.

Hedge Delta (Binomial) Hedging
$$\Delta = \frac{f_u - f_d}{S_0 u - S_0 d}$$
Variables

$f_u, f_d$ : option price in each scenario. $\Delta$ : quantity of asset to hold.

Intuition

How many assets to buy so that the portfolio value is the same whether the market rises or falls.

Example

$\Delta = \frac{1-0}{22-18.2} = 0.262$ : buy 0.262 shares to hedge the call.

Link with BS

In BS, $\Delta = N(d_1)$ — it is the "Greek" Delta, sensitivity of price to the underlying.

Risk-Neutral Probability (Binomial) Risk-Neutral Pricing
$$p = \frac{e^{rT} - d}{u - d}$$
Variables

$r$ : risk-free rate, $T$ : period, $u$ : up factor, $d$ : down factor

Intuition

This is NOT the real market probability, but the "risk-neutral" probability such that the expectation of $S_T$ yields the risk-free rate.

Price formula

$f = e^{-rT}[p f_u + (1-p)f_d]$ — expectation under $\mathbb{Q}$, discounted.

Calibration of u, d

$u = e^{\sigma\sqrt{T}}$, $d = e^{-\sigma\sqrt{T}}$ — link with historical volatility.

Put-Call Parity No-Arbitrage
$$c + Ke^{-rT} = p + S_0$$
Variables

$c, p$ : call and put prices, $K$ : strike, $r$ : rate, $T$ : maturity, $S_0$ : spot price

Interpretation

Call + secured cash = Put + asset. Both portfolios have the same value at T.

14Possible Exam Questions
🟢 Easy Level
What is the difference between a European and an American option? ▾
A European option can only be exercised at maturity $T$. An American option can be exercised at any time until $T$ inclusive. In practice, most listed options are American, but we use European options in theory because they are simpler to price (Black-Scholes formula applicable). An American option is always worth at least as much as a European option with the same characteristics, because it offers more flexibility.
What are the 6 variables that influence the price of an option? ▾
(1) $S_0$ : spot price of the underlying — the higher it is, the more expensive the call. (2) $K$ : strike — the lower the strike, the more expensive the call. (3) $T$ : maturity — more time = more value (time value). (4) $\sigma$ : volatility — increasing $\sigma$ raises the value of both (call and put). (5) $r$ : risk-free rate — impact via discounting of strike $Ke^{-rT}$. (6) Dividends — dividends reduce the stock price → unfavorable for calls.
🔵 Intermediate Level
Prove the put-call parity. What does a violation of this relation imply? ▾
Proof: Consider two portfolios:
— A : call $c$ + cash $Ke^{-rT}$ → value at T: if $S_T > K$ : $(S_T - K) + K = S_T$ ; if $S_T \leq K$ : $K$. → $\max(S_T, K)$.
— C : put $p$ + asset $S_0$ → value at T: if $S_T < K$ : $(K - S_T) + S_T = K$ ; if $S_T \geq K$ : $S_T$. → $\max(S_T, K)$.
Same value at T → by no-arbitrage: $c + Ke^{-rT} = p + S_0$.
Violation: If $c + Ke^{-rT} > p + S_0$, we sell A and buy C (positive risk-free profit). Arbitrageurs immediately correct the imbalance.
Explain the delta-hedging reasoning in the binomial tree. Why $\Delta = (f_u - f_d)/(S_0 u - S_0 d)$? ▾
The idea is to construct a portfolio made of $\Delta$ long shares and one short call, such that the final value is identical regardless of the outcome.<
— If up : $P_u = S_0 u \cdot \Delta - f_u$<
— If down : $P_d = S_0 d \cdot \Delta - f_d$<
We impose $P_u = P_d$ : $S_0 u \Delta - f_u = S_0 d \Delta - f_d$ → $\Delta(S_0 u - S_0 d) = f_u - f_d$ → $\Delta = \frac{f_u - f_d}{S_0(u-d)}$.
This risk-free portfolio grows at rate $r$ → we can compute $f$ by backward induction.
🔴 Hard Level
What is implied volatility? Why does the volatility smile contradict Black-Scholes, and what are the implications? ▾
Implied volatility is the value of $\sigma$ which, plugged into the BS formula, reproduces the market price of the option. It is therefore a residual: everything that BS does not capture is absorbed into $\sigma_{impl}$.

Contradiction with BS: In the BS model, $\sigma$ is an intrinsic property of the asset, constant. It should therefore not depend on the strike $K$ or the maturity $T$. Yet in practice, implied vols vary with $K$ (smile) and $T$ (term structure), forming a volatility surface.<

Implications: (1) Real distribution tails are thicker than the Gaussian (leptokurtosis). (2) OTM (put) options are more expensive than predicted by BS → risk premium for protection. (3) We must use alternative models: Heston (stochastic volatility), local vol model, SABR, etc. (4) The vol surface is a trading tool: it is used to interpolate/extrapolate prices and to compute Greeks in a more realistic way.
Show that the lower bound of the call, $c \geq S_0 - Ke^{-rT}$, is consistent with Black-Scholes when $\sigma \to 0$. ▾
When $\sigma \to 0$ and assuming $S_0 > K$ (in-the-money):<
$d_1 = \frac{\ln(S_0/K) + rT}{\sigma\sqrt{T}} + \frac{\sigma\sqrt{T}}{2} \to +\infty$ → $N(d_1) \to 1$<
$d_2 = d_1 - \sigma\sqrt{T} \to +\infty$ → $N(d_2) \to 1$<
Thus $c = S_0 N(d_1) - Ke^{-rT}N(d_2) \to S_0 - Ke^{-rT}$ ✓<

If $S_0 < K$ (out-of-the-money) and $\sigma \to 0$ : $d_1 \to -\infty$, $d_2 \to -\infty$ → $N(d_1), N(d_2) \to 0$ → $c \to 0 \geq \max(S_0 - Ke^{-rT}, 0)$ ✓
15Classic Pitfalls
⚡
Confusion right vs obligation: The buyer of an option has a right. The seller (writer) has an obligation. Never invert them. A long call cannot lose more than the premium, but a short call can lose infinitely.
⚡
Moneyness inverted between call and put: A call is "in the money" when $S_0 > K$, but a put is "in the money" when $S_0 < K$. Do not confuse the two directions.
⚡
$p$ in the binomial ≠ real probability: The probability $p = (e^{rT} - d)/(u-d)$ is a risk-neutral probability (measure $\mathbb{Q}$), not the historical probability $\mathbb{P}$. The price obtained is correct regardless of investors' risk preferences.
⚡
The upper bound of the put is $K$, not $Ke^{-rT}$: $p \leq K$ (non-discounted value) because that is the max payoff if $S_T = 0$, received at maturity T. The lower bound is discounted: $Ke^{-rT} - S_0$.
⚡
Volatility and price: both (call AND put) increase with $\sigma$: Unlike other variables, a rise in volatility increases the value of calls AND puts, because both benefit from greater dispersion.
⚡
Black-Scholes only applies to European vanilla options: For exotic (barrier, binary, lookback…) or American options, one cannot use BS directly. The binomial tree remains the universal tool.
⚡
Oscillating convergence of the binomial: The binomial price is not monotone in $n$ — it oscillates around the BS value. For even vs odd $n$, prices alternate slightly. Do not conclude there is an error if the price at $n=2$ is far from BS.
16Final Revision Sheet

📌 Key Definitions

  • Call : right to buy at $K$
  • Put : right to sell at $K$
  • Premium : price of the option ($c$ or $p$)
  • Strike : exercise price $K$
  • Maturity : expiration date $T$
  • ATM : $S_0 = K$
  • ITM call : $S_0 > K$
  • OTM call : $S_0 < K$

📐 Essential Formulas

  • Call payoff : $\max(S_T - K, 0)$
  • Put payoff : $\max(K - S_T, 0)$
  • PCP : $c + Ke^{-rT} = p + S_0$
  • Call bound : $S_0 - Ke^{-rT} \leq c \leq S_0$
  • Put bound : $Ke^{-rT} - S_0 \leq p \leq K$
  • Risk-neutral $p$ : $(e^{rT}-d)/(u-d)$
  • $\Delta$ : $(f_u - f_d)/(S_0 u - S_0 d)$
  • BS call : $S_0 N(d_1) - Ke^{-rT}N(d_2)$

🧠 Must memorize

  • Short call = unlimited risk
  • $\sigma\uparrow$ → call ↑ AND put ↑
  • $p$ in binomial = risk-neutral probability
  • BS convergence: binomial $\to$ BS as $n \to \infty$
  • Volatility smile = BS is a model, not reality
  • American option ≥ European option

📊 Key Strategies

  • Bull Spread : bullish, limited gain/loss
  • Butterfly : low vol, profit if $S_T \approx K_2$
  • Protective Put : long S + long put → insurance
  • Covered Call : long S + short call → income
  • Straddle : long call + long put → vol bet

⚙️ Binomial vs BS

  • Binomial : universal, all option types
  • BS : closed-form, European vanilla only
  • Exotics → binomial mandatory
  • Americans → binomial mandatory

🎯 Model Limits

  • BS assumes $\sigma$ constant → false in practice
  • Volatility smile reveals fat tails
  • Real distributions = leptokurtic
  • Extensions: Heston, SABR, local vol
✨ Key Message for the Examiner

Options are instruments with asymmetric risk: the buyer benefits from a right, the seller bears an obligation. All pricing rests on the principle of no-arbitrage: two portfolios with the same future payoff must have the same price today. The binomial tree operationalizes this principle in an intuitive and recursive way; Black-Scholes is its elegant continuous limit. The volatility smile reminds us that all models are wrong — some are useful.
